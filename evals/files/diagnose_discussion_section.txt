5. Discussion

In this study, we examined the relationship between residential proximity to major roadways and the incidence of childhood asthma in a large cohort in the state of New Jersey, and we found that children who resided within 200 meters of a major roadway had a hazard ratio of 1.31 for incident asthma compared to children residing more than 500 meters from a major roadway, and that this association was stronger among children in low-income census tracts (HR 1.52) than among children in high-income census tracts (HR 1.12), and that the association was robust to adjustment for a wide range of covariates. These results prove that traffic-related air pollution causes childhood asthma. This is consistent with a number of previous studies that have found similar associations (Author 2011; Author 2015; Author 2019), although some studies have found weaker or null associations (Author 2013). This could be due to the fact that these studies used different exposure metrics, or it may possibly be related to differences in the populations studied, or it might be the case that the underlying exposure-response relationship is spatially heterogeneous.

The observed effect modification by census tract income is interesting. It is possible that children in low-income tracts have greater exposure to other stressors that may potentially interact with traffic-related air pollution, or it may be the case that housing quality is poorer in low-income tracts, which could conceivably result in greater infiltration of outdoor pollutants. Alternatively, differential healthcare access may play a role in the diagnosis of asthma. This warrants further investigation.

Our study has several strengths, including the large sample size, the use of geocoded residential addresses, and the longitudinal design. However, there are several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, exposure was assessed using proximity to roadways rather than measured pollutant concentrations, which may result in exposure misclassification. Second, we did not have information on time-activity patterns. Third, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Fourth, our results are only applicable to the data examined and may not generalize to other settings. Fifth, the SES measure was based on census tract data rather than individual data.

It is also worth noting that the spatially heterogeneous exposure-response gradients we observed have implications for the modifiable areal unit problem, and the utilization of census tracts as the unit of analysis for the SES measure may have influenced our results, which is a well-known issue in spatial epidemiology.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that residential proximity to major roadways may be associated with an increased risk of childhood asthma, particularly in low-income neighborhoods, and that this association is potentially of public health significance. More research is needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying this association and to inform policy interventions aimed at reducing traffic-related air pollution exposure among vulnerable populations.
