# Review, reviewers, and coauthors

Purpose: get useful criticism from colleagues and from journals, revise and answer it well, write fair reviews of other people's work, and run a coauthored manuscript without losing the draft or the collaborator.

Lead source: Heard (chs. 22, 23, 24, 26, and the peer-review part of ch. 25). Supplements: Alley (Lesson 10 on soliciting criticism and on collaborative writing, Lesson 7 on describing others' shortcomings) and Zinsser (ch. 24 on editors). Self-revision before any of this belongs to revision.md.

## Friendly review: choosing the reviewer

**Pick reviewers who represent your target audience.** A close colleague first, then someone further afield after a revision round, because the second reader stands where most of your readers will stand. (Heard, pp. 204-205) [firm]

**Cultivate the critical reviewers, not the flattering ones, then thank them and reciprocate.** You benefit most from a reviewer who is unafraid to raise issues that require hard work of you. (Heard, p. 205) [firm]

**Do not seek too many critics.** Alley's case: the Department of Energy sent a five-year plan to 200 reviewers, and the report was scrapped after years of haggling. (Alley, p. 221) [firm]

## Friendly review: making the job easy

**Polish before you send.** A reviewer's attention spent on typos is attention not spent on your argument. Two exceptions: an early structural question you flag explicitly as such, and a negotiated mutual rough-draft arrangement. (Heard, pp. 205-206) [firm]

**Allow three to four weeks.** Heard is direct about the alternative: "If you have a deadline closer than that, it's your problem, not your reviewer's" (p. 206). [firm]

**Send an editable format and a PDF, and offer paper or electronic.** A PDF alone "is awkward to edit" (Heard, p. 206). [firm]

**Number the lines and pages so the reviewer can point precisely.** Heard also asks for double spacing (p. 206), which matters less under format-free submission but costs nothing. [firm]

**Ask specific, concrete questions.** You get a more useful review at no extra reviewing burden, because the reviewer spends the same hours on better-aimed work. (Heard, pp. 206-207) [firm]

A request, weak then strong (example: ours):

- Weak: "Any comments welcome, especially on the analysis."
- Strong: "Two things I am unsure of: does the Results section make clear why we used a spatial lag rather than a spatial error model, and does the Discussion overclaim in the third paragraph?"

**Remind at two to three weeks, and again two weeks later, with an offer of help. Do not nag.** (Heard, p. 207) [firm]

## Friendly review: reading what comes back

**Read the review once, then do nothing for a day or two.** Heard's reason: "Few, if any, of us are capable of reacting calmly and constructively to criticism of our work (however spot-on it may be) on first reading" (p. 207). [firm]

**Sort every comment into one of three categories** (Heard, pp. 207-209) [firm]:

1. Clear problems with easy fixes. Do these first, to ease back into the manuscript.
2. Comments that are onto something without an obvious path. Set a high bar for keeping your original approach, and do not refuse a fix merely because it is laborious.
3. Comments that make you angry.

**Treat a category-three comment as a failure of your clarity, not of the reader.** Heard: "It's your job to make your writing clear even for a reader who is in a rush, distracted, careless, or even (gasp) not quite as brilliant as you" (p. 209). [firm]

**Discuss category-two comments with your reviewer, but do not argue.** Heard adds the practical reason: "at least, not if you want future reviews from the same person" (pp. 208-209). [firm]

**Act on criticism you asked for.** Alley's reading of the angry critic is useful: agitated critics are frustrated readers, not attackers, and their agitation is a compliment to the importance of what you wrote. If a criticism seems odd, mull it over rather than dismiss it, because a deeper problem may sit under it that the critic could not name. (Alley, pp. 220-221) [firm]

## Serial review and your own error list

**With each new draft, supply a short summary of changes and answer every substantive comment.** Never ignore one. Heard: "Nothing raises a supervisor's blood pressure more quickly than correcting the same apparent mistake in a third consecutive draft!" (pp. 209-210). [firm]

**Before the next manuscript, re-read the revisions made to the last one and apply them unprompted.** Keep a growing checklist of your own recurring mistakes. Making a mistake once is evidence that you are prone to it. (Heard, p. 210) [firm]

## Formal review: before the decision

**Do not treat peer review as an obstacle.** Doing so "sacrifices much available writing help" (Heard, pp. 212-213). A good reviewer distinguishes not publishable, not publishable yet, and not publishable in this journal, and you gain from each. [firm]

**Suggest favored reviewers who review a lot, whose work you admire, and who do not know your work intimately.** Heard is explicit about what you are not doing: "You aren't looking for someone who will recommend acceptance of your manuscript regardless of its quality" (p. 215). [firm]

**Disfavor reviewers sparingly.** Wanting more than two on one submission, or any on more than the occasional submission, is a sign to examine how you relate to colleagues in your field. (Heard, pp. 215-216) [firm]

## Formal review: after the decision

**Read the reviews even after a decline.** Ignoring them throws away free help, dismisses a good-faith effort, and ignores the fact that the reviewer pool is small enough that the same person may see the manuscript again. (Heard, p. 218) [firm]

**Take "major revision" as major.** The decision does not guarantee acceptance. (Heard, pp. 218-219) [firm]

**Write to the editor when it matters: to negotiate a deadline, to clarify a cryptic comment, or to test a proposed fix.** Use email, and be gracious and constructive. Heard is contacted on 5 to 10% of manuscripts he handles and calls that about right. (Heard, pp. 219-221) [firm]

- Ask: "would approach X satisfy this objection?"
- Never ask: "if I take approach X, will my manuscript be accepted?" (Heard, p. 220) [firm]

**Do not appeal a decline.** Genuine editorial error is rare, appeals rarely succeed, they risk a reputation for being troublesome, and they are slower than revising and submitting elsewhere. (Heard, p. 221) [firm]

## Revising after review

**Ask what change, however major, would best address the comment.** Not what the easiest change would be. Reviewers notice when a two-thousand-word review produces two new sentences and a few corrected spellings. (Heard, pp. 222-223) [firm]

**Assume the reviewers are probably right.** Heard: "reviewers aren't always right, but it is wise to think that they probably are" (p. 224). They are often more experienced, and you have an unconscious investment in your own choices. [firm]

**Make a merely equivalent change anyway.** It costs nothing and buys goodwill, which Heard calls chits you can cash when you really need to resist a suggestion. (p. 224) [firm]

**Never quietly ignore a suggestion.** "your ploy is sure to be detected" (Heard, p. 224). [firm]

**Prefer rebutting while revising to rebutting and changing nothing.** A reviewer's misunderstanding marks a clarity fault other readers will share, and refusing to revise requires arguing that this misreading was uniquely incorrect, which Heard calls "actually quite improbable" (pp. 224-225). [firm]

A category-two comment and two replies (example: ours):

- Comment: "The authors' choice of a 5 km neighborhood threshold seems arbitrary."
- Rebut and change nothing: "The threshold follows standard practice in this literature."
- Rebut but revise: "The threshold was set by the median inter-site distance, not by convention. We agree the original text did not say so, and we have added the derivation and a sensitivity check at 3 and 8 km."

**Defer to the editor at most once per manuscript, and do not bluff.** (Heard, p. 225) [firm]

## The Response to Reviews

**Paste the reviews into the Response before you start revising, label them, and record each change as you make it.** The Response then "doubles as a checklist for revision" (Heard, p. 227). [firm]

**Use three parts: brief thanks, a short summary of the major changes, then the full text of every comment interleaved with point-by-point replies.** (Heard, p. 226) [firm] The opening paragraph of Heard's own real Response thanks the reviewers, names the two substantive additions, reports a 10% cut in length, and states the typographic convention separating comments from replies (pp. 226-227).

**Reply to every comment, group trivia under one line, and do not reorganize the reviews.** Keeping the reviewers' order lets the editor see that nothing was dropped. A single "All corrected, thanks" handles a list of typographic fixes. (Heard, p. 227) [firm]

**Keep the Response polite and constructive even when you are offended.** It will probably be sent to the reviewers. (Heard, pp. 227-228) [firm]

**When you rebut, take the blame.** Heard advises admitting, and even exaggerating slightly, your own part in allowing the reviewer's mistake (p. 228). [firm]

**Put substantive science in the manuscript, not in the Response.** Heard's test: "if the editor needs them to accept the correctness of your approach, readers will need them, too" (pp. 229-230). [firm]

**Self-revise the Response, paying particular attention to tone.** (Heard, p. 230) [firm]

**Line-number references or quoted new text is a real split.** Heard names "two schools of thought" and prefers line numbers (p. 229). [judgment]

Heard's three worked exchanges (pp. 226-229), in short form:

- Accepting. The reviewer suggests a nearest-neighbor test. The reply concedes in two sentences and points to the new Table 2.
- Rebutting while revising. The reviewer misreads the columns of a contingency table. The reply opens "While the reviewer is mistaken here, we appreciate the comment because our careless writing led to the misunderstanding" (p. 228), then lists the clarified wording in Methods, Results, and Discussion by line number.
- Partly adopting, and deferring. The authors add the requested STRUCTURE analysis, keep the neighbor-joining tree with a stated reason, and close by offering to move one analysis to an appendix if the editors strongly prefer it.

## Writing a review for someone else

**Write dispassionate, concrete comments located by line number.** Heard's rule for the whole job: "do unto those you review as you would have them do unto you" (pp. 243-244). [firm]

**Separate what precludes publication from what would merely improve the paper.** The editor needs that distinction to make a decision. (Heard, pp. 243-244) [firm]

Heard's review comment, weak to strong (pp. 243-244):

- Of little value: "The statistical analysis is wrong."
- Good: "The statistical test at line 377 appears problematic because the assumption of normality of residuals is likely violated for this kind of data."
- Excellent: the same sentence plus a pointer to a published treatment, plus a named alternative, such as a model with Poisson errors or a randomization test.

**Never share a manuscript you are reviewing.** If you want a teaching example, ask colleagues for reviews they have received. (Heard, p. 243) [firm, ethics]

**Weigh signing against your career stage.** Signing invites clarifying contact and builds relationships; retaliation is rare but not unheard of; before hiring or tenure it may be wisest to remain anonymous by default. (Heard, p. 244) [judgment]

**In a published Comment, critique the science without attacking the author.** Heard contrasts two 2013 Comments, Janssen's and Bizarro's, on exactly this line. (Heard, p. 144) [firm]

## Working with editors

**Value the editor's objective eye.** Zinsser lists what a good editor actually does: "pruning, shaping, clarifying," fixing inconsistencies of tense, pronoun, location, and tone, catching sentences that can be read two ways, splitting long awkward sentences, restoring the main road when the writer has wandered, building the missing transitions, and questioning judgment and taste (Zinsser, pp. 290-291). [firm]

**An editor owes the reader comprehension.** Zinsser: "An editor should never allow something to get into print that he doesn't understand. If he doesn't understand it, at least one other person won't, and that's one too many" (p. 292). [firm] The generic "he" is 2001 usage, not a rule to copy.

**Resist changes to your style; refuse changes to your content.** Zinsser calls tampering with a writer's opinions "the cardinal sin," and warns: "If you allow your distinctiveness to be edited out, you will lose one of your main virtues" (pp. 292-293). [Zinsser's judgment, not a science-specific rule.] In a journal the editor is also a gatekeeper, which Zinsser's magazine setting is not, so for a scientific manuscript Heard's revise-and-rebut procedure above governs.

**If an editor strikes your first person, disagree politely on reasoned grounds, accept the edit for that document, and keep the habit.** Alley notes that editors tire of fattening sentences to remove it (p. 57). The first-person ruling itself is in sentences.md. [firm]

## Coauthorship: the conversation

**Use published authorship guidelines and have an explicit conversation.** Heard: "You have to do both" (pp. 249-250), because which guidelines apply is ambiguous, practice varies by country and field, and every guideline rests on vague words like "substantial." [firm]

**Hold that conversation as early as possible in the research, and write down who does what and what authorship follows.** (Heard, p. 250) [firm]

**Never accept coauthorship of a paper you do not thoroughly understand.** Heard: "If work is flawed or fraudulent, every coauthor will share the stain whether or not they were aware of the problem" (p. 250). [firm]

**Defuse the awkwardness by blaming the general ambiguity, not the collaborator.** (Heard, p. 251) [judgment]

**Ask a senior colleague what your field's author-order convention is.** It varies by field, country, journal, and era, so no general rule helps. (Heard, p. 252) [firm]

## Coauthorship: dividing the writing

**Agree on the hypothesis and the results before drafting anything else.** Alley takes this from George Whitesides: every other section is wasted effort until the team agrees on those two cornerstones. (Alley, p. 214) [firm]

**Do not write literally together, and do not split the document into equal member-written parts.** Heard's reason for the first: dividing the work and exchanging drafts is faster (p. 253). Alley's reason for the second: equal shares produce disjointed styles with no focus, no transitions, and no background (p. 221). [firm in both]

**Name a lead writer, who need not be the first author.** The lead writer shepherds the team, holds the dated version of record, enforces consistency, and corresponds with the journal (Heard, pp. 253-256). Alley adds the selection criterion: someone who works ahead of schedule (p. 222). Early-career researchers make good lead writers because their incentive is strongest. [firm]

**Expect everyone to bend.** No coauthor will read the final text as the text they would have written. (Alley, p. 222) [firm]

**Divide tasks to use complementary strengths, in parallel by section or in series by stage, on a realistic schedule.** (Heard, pp. 254-255) [firm]

**Put questions to cowriters as comments in the text, not in separate messages.** (Heard, p. 256) [firm]

**Get every coauthor's approval before submission and before resubmission.** Journals email every listed coauthor, and one denial returns the manuscript. For a large team, a message with a deadline and default assent is acceptable. (Heard, pp. 256-257) [firm]

**Write and sign a publishing power-of-attorney now, and tell your collaborators where it is.** An unreachable coauthor can hold a manuscript in limbo and damage a junior colleague's career, and Heard notes that most researchers dismiss the idea until the need arrives, by which time it is too late (pp. 257-258). His model letter names a colleague, sets a 60-day unavailability trigger, fixes a date range, and names an institutional officer who can confirm the absence (Box 26.1, p. 258). [firm]

## Criticizing other people's work in print

**Describe others' shortcomings respectfully.** Alley's reason is practical as well as decent: one of those researchers will likely review your paper. (Alley, p. 118) [firm]

**Remember what citation is.** Heard: "There is something of a social compact among scientists: I cite your work in part because I would very much like you to cite mine" (p. 133). How to choose citations is in structure.md. [firm]

## Checklist

1. Does the friendly reviewer represent the paper's target audience, and is the draft polished enough that comments will land on the argument? (Heard, pp. 204-206)
2. Did the request carry specific questions, line numbers, an editable file, and three to four weeks? (Heard, pp. 206-207)
3. Has every comment been sorted into the three categories, with the angry-making ones treated as clarity failures? (Heard, pp. 207-209)
4. Does the revision answer every comment, including the ones being rebutted? (Heard, p. 224)
5. For each rebuttal, is there still a revision that removes the cause of the misreading? (Heard, pp. 224-225)
6. Does the Response follow the three parts, reply to every comment in the reviewers' order, and stay polite enough to be forwarded? (Heard, pp. 226-228)
7. Is every piece of substantive science in the manuscript rather than only in the Response? (Heard, pp. 229-230)
8. In a review you are writing, is each comment concrete, line-numbered, and marked as either blocking or improving? (Heard, pp. 243-244)
9. Has the authorship conversation happened, in writing, and does every coauthor understand the work they are signing? (Heard, pp. 249-250)
10. Is there one named lead writer holding one dated version of record? (Heard, pp. 253-256; Alley, p. 222)
11. Has every coauthor approved this submission or resubmission? (Heard, pp. 256-257)
12. Does every description of someone else's work criticize the science rather than the author? (Alley, p. 118; Heard, p. 144)
