# Sources: the four books, what each contributes, and the rulings

The skill is built from four books and nothing else. Reader notes for the three long books
(about 17,000 to 20,000 words each, with page references) sit in the build workspace at
`~/.claude/skills/write-science-well-workspace/notes/`; Strunk's full text is in
`.../workspace/sources/strunk_gutenberg.txt`. Page conventions: Strunk is cited by rule
number or chapter V entry; Alley, Heard, and Zinsser by printed page.

## Strunk, The Elements of Style (1918; Harcourt 1920)

A 43-page manual for Cornell students: seven rules of usage, eleven principles of
composition, a chapter on form, and an alphabetical list of misused words. There is no E. B.
White material; the edition in the folder predates the 1959 revision.

What the skill takes: the paragraph as the unit of composition with a topic sentence (rules 8
and 9); the active voice with the topic test that decides when the passive is right (rule
10); statements in positive form (11); definite, specific, concrete language (12); omit
needless words, with the list of phrases to cut and "the fact that" (13); avoid a succession
of loose sentences (14); parallel form for coordinate ideas (15); related words together,
"only" next to what it modifies (16); one tense in a summary (17); the emphatic word at the
end of the sentence (18); the comma rules that change meaning (3 to 6); the participial
phrase that must refer to the subject (7); and the still-valid entries of chapter V (case,
character, nature, factor, feature, system, respectively, very, so, interesting, one of the
most, along these lines, kind of, less and fewer, like and as, while).

Historical, not applied: generic "he" for every antecedent (chapter V, "They"); shall and
will; the disfavor of the split infinitive; "however" never first in its sentence; "student
body"; the spelling and syllabication lists; "to-day" with a hyphen.

## Zinsser, On Writing Well (25th anniversary edition, 2001)

A craft book for anyone who writes nonfiction, written in the first person by a working
writer and teacher. Thesis: clear writing is clear thinking made visible; the method is
subtraction; what survives the subtraction is the writer's voice.

What the skill takes: clutter and its taxonomy, the four adulterants, the bracket method,
the expectation that a first draft can lose half its words (chapters 2 and 3); the four
articles of faith, clarity, simplicity, brevity, humanity (chapter 16); little qualifiers
and hedges as erosions of trust, active and precise verbs, adverbs and adjectives that
repeat, concept nouns, creeping nounism, mood changers at the start of a sentence, that
versus which, the Othello and Iago antecedent trap, paragraphs short but not chopped
(chapter 10); the unities of pronoun, tense, and mood, the six questions before starting,
one provocative thought per piece, thinking small (chapter 8); the lead and the ending,
escaping the recapitulating "Roman-numeral III" conclusion, stopping when you are ready
(chapter 9); the Science and Technology chapter in full: the reader knows nothing, the
upside-down pyramid that starts at the one fact the reader must have and widens into
significance, the reader's-next-question device, plain style as a requirement for peers and
not only for laymen, "won't, not can't" (chapter 15); rewriting as reshaping, reading aloud
for the links, the quickest fix (chapter 10); the tyranny of the final product, fear,
enjoyment as a switch, never accept the expert's first answer, never publish what you do not
understand (chapters 12, 21, 22); a writer's decisions on a long piece, one thought per
sentence, after every sentence ask what the reader wants next (chapter 23).

Left out: the interview, travel, memoir, humor, sports, and arts chapters, except the rules
named above that transfer; the 1990s pronoun advice (take the repair techniques, drop the
conclusion; singular "they" is now standard); most usage verdicts of chapter 7 (treat the
chapter as a method: does the word fill a need and convey a picture?); the technology
references.

## Alley, The Craft of Scientific Writing (4th edition, Springer 2018)

Ten lessons for engineers and scientists, plus appendices on grammar, punctuation, usage,
and format. Thesis: style follows from an analysis of audience, purpose, and occasion; the
purposes are to inform and persuade, not to impress; balance precision against clarity and
strip needless complexity.

What the skill takes: audience, purpose, occasion (Lesson 1); precision versus clarity,
dictionary not thesaurus, no synonyms for variety, never exaggerate, average sentence length
in the teens, one idea per sentence, an early clue for a long sentence, no stacked
modifiers, no needless abbreviations or slashes (Lesson 2); ambiguity from word choice, word
order, pronouns, and punctuation, the standalone "this" ban, the comma after every
introductory phrase (Lesson 3); verbs early, verbs buried in nouns, "there is" cut in
revision, the natural voice, first person judiciously and not sentence-initial, concrete
nouns and the abstract-noun list, redundancies, writing zeroes, phrase reductions, the
crippling "very", bureaucratic waste (Lesson 4); the connection rule for sentence openers
and the three tiers of openers, equations as grammatical parts of sentences, illustrations
matched to the text and placed after their reference (Lesson 5); first sentences that orient
and step, the three failed beginnings, mapping, definitions, jargon efficient only for these
readers, examples and analogies, scale anchors (Lesson 6); titles as scope specification,
the four introduction questions, the literature review that shows gaps, organizing
strategies, descriptive parallel headings, never a single subsection, paragraph length
bands, conclusions that reach a plateau (Lesson 7); emphasis by wording, repetition, and
placement where text borders white space, the informative versus descriptive summary,
limits on lists and the case against bullets, appendices (Lesson 8); proposals: two-part
organization, show don't tell the problem, the four plan questions, the three winning
strategies (Lesson 9); the four-stage process, rabbits and turtles, the working outline, a
page a day, writer's block as three stoppages, revising with sleep and a changed look, the
lead writer for teams, finishing as correcting (Lesson 10); the three-tier severity of usage
errors and the usage entries (Appendix C); numerals rules (Appendix D).

Left out: the email and instructions lessons; typography and layout; the Gunning Fog
arithmetic (the target is mentioned once as advisory); the US baseball and Space Shuttle
exhibits are kept only where the structural point needs them. Errata in the text: the
Challenger date on p. 1 (1986, not 1996); the eruption date in Table 5-2 (May 18); the US
quotation-mark rule is stated backwards on pp. 10 and 247 while its examples are right.

## Heard, The Scientist's Guide to Writing (Princeton 2016)

Twenty-eight chapters for students and early-career scientists across the natural sciences.
Thesis: writing is a craft learned by deliberate practice, its goal is clarity so complete
it feels like telepathy, and writing behavior matters as much as the written product.

What the skill takes: reading as a writer, the Big Six behavioral challenges, getting
started, early writing, momentum, loose threads, swooping, placeholders, writer's block from
inside (chapters 3 to 6); the story, the two-sentence mini-summary, symptoms of a missing
story, wordstack and concept map and outlines, figure shuffling, selling the story and the
timid framings to reject (chapter 7); IMRaD as a finding system and the hourglass (8); the
title as advertisement, the abstract that gives the result away (9); the introduction's
three moves, breadth by venue, ending on the main result (10); Methods organized for the
reader with the credibility criterion for detail and the copyright case against reusing
wording (11); Results stripped to what the reader needs, the pattern named, numbers in text
versus table versus figure, precision, table and figure design, legends, pointing at
graphics (12); the Discussion that turns data into knowledge, its four components, hedges,
the weaknesses paragraph, specific future work, usually no separate conclusion (13);
supplements and the three-way placement test, citations as content choices (14, 15);
deviations from IMRaD (16); paragraphs unified, coherent, distinct, the seven schemes,
relational devices, power positions (17); grammar as convention, tense by section and by
citation attitude, the case for the active voice and the three jobs of the passive, actor
next to action, topic and stress, noun strings, stack-loading (18); word meaning as
convention, technical terms, coinages, condensed words, nominalizations (19); brevity, the
eleven text-bulgers, the worked 30 percent cut (20); self-revision in single-purpose rounds,
reader simulation, murder your darlings (21); friendly review, formal review, the Response
to Reviews, writing a review, coauthorship and the lead writer, the publishing
power-of-attorney (22 to 26); writing in English as an additional language (27); clarity
first, then pleasure (28).

Left out: blogs; the pre-2016 counts and tool names; the argument that low replication rates
are not a handicap, which a 2026 reader should weigh against a decade of reporting reform
(the credibility criterion stays for readability; the venue's checklist adds what it adds);
chapter 28's case for whimsy, which the author himself flags as personal and possibly wrong.

## Precedence

The author's live instruction > the project's own conventions (style sheet, term list, venue
rules) > house-style.md > the four books.
Among the books, for scientific documents: Alley and Heard over Zinsser over Strunk. Alley
and Heard write for scientists and engineers and know the venues; Zinsser writes for all
nonfiction and supplies the diagnosis of clutter and the discipline of the lead and the
ending; Strunk supplies the baseline of usage and composition that the other three assume.

## The rulings, with reasons

1. **Hedging.** Zinsser cuts qualifiers because each "whittles away some fraction of the
   reader's trust" (p. 71). Heard keeps hedges that express the precise strength of a claim
   and cuts the excess, which lengthens text and makes the argument hard to take seriously
   (pp. 184 to 185). Alley attacks inflation rather than caution: "prove", "optimal",
   "clearly demonstrate", "unambiguous" invite rebuttal (pp. 19 to 20, 163 to 164). Ruling:
   hedge exactly to the evidence, cut habit hedges, and treat inflation as the equal fault.
   The house-style starter rule agrees: certainty for designed behavior, hedges only for
   what the analysis declines to resolve.
2. **Passive voice.** Strunk: use the active, but the need to make a particular word the
   subject often decides the voice (rule 10). Alley: choose the natural voice, usually
   active; passive when it keeps focus on the study's subject (pp. 53 to 54). Heard: active
   unless the passive demonstrably serves the sentence, for three jobs: directing attention,
   obscuring an actor nobody cares about, avoiding a complex subject (p. 166). Zinsser:
   active unless there is no comfortable way around the passive (p. 68). Ruling: active by
   default; passive for Heard's three jobs and Strunk's topic test.
3. **First person.** Zinsser urges "I" and concedes the valid bans (pp. 21 to 22). Alley:
   judicious, emphasis on the work, never open a sentence with "I" or "we", enter through
   an infinitive phrase (pp. 55 to 57). Heard: "trees don't fell themselves" (p. 165). House
   style: measured "we" as the actor, the verb naming the action, never to narrate drafting.
   Ruling: measured "we", not sentence-initial by default, never to narrate the writing.
4. **Sentence-initial "However" and "Because".** Strunk bans the first (chapter V) and
   Zinsser calls it a wet dishrag (p. 74); Alley reports that almost all editors accept it
   (p. 238) and calls the ban on "Because" a myth no grammar book supports (pp. 78, 139).
   Ruling: both allowed. An author's written-out argument turns ("However, that does not
   mean…") stay when you edit the author's own text.
5. **"Data".** Strunk: plural (chapter V). Alley: singular is acceptable to many; write
   "data point" to sidestep conservatives (p. 254). Ruling: plural in formal prose, or
   sidestep.
6. **Bullets.** Alley: bullets remove hierarchy and therefore emphasis, show no connections,
   and are first-draft thinking; at most one vertical list per two or three pages (pp. 146
   to 148). Heard is silent. House style: paste-ready prose carries no bullets. Ruling: none
   in manuscript prose; numbered lists in instructions.
7. **Semicolons and em dashes.** Strunk makes the semicolon the mark for two related
   independent clauses (rule 5); Zinsser uses it sparingly and admits he breaks the rule
   (p. 73); Alley calls it fussy (p. 246). Zinsser and Alley both use the em dash freely.
   House style bans the em dash in manuscript prose. Ruling: semicolon for two closely
   linked clauses, not as a habit; em dash never.
8. **The end of an introduction.** Heard names the disagreement among guides and includes
   the main result (p. 87). Alley maps the document at the end of a long document's
   introduction (pp. 120 to 121). Ruling: a paper ends on the question, the approach, and
   the main result; a long document maps its sections.
9. **Methods detail.** Heard: include a detail if and only if it could change the reader's
   interpretation of the results; replication-level detail costs every reader for a
   hypothetical few (pp. 94 to 95). Reporting checklists since 2016 push the other way.
   Ruling: the credibility criterion for the prose, plus what the venue's checklist
   requires.
10. **Conclusions.** Heard: usually skip a separate conclusion; end the Discussion on the
    most important finding and its significance (pp. 124 to 125). Alley, writing for
    reports: a conclusion that analyzes the results as a whole, gives a future perspective,
    introduces no new evidence, and reaches a plateau (pp. 131 to 132). Zinsser: escape the
    recap; when you are ready to stop, stop (pp. 64 to 66). House style: a chapter closes as
    a conclusion with scope, the changed claim, the limits, the consequence. Ruling: papers
    per Heard unless the venue wants a conclusion; chapters, reports, and proposals per
    Alley and the house rule, never a recap.
11. **Repetition.** Alley repeats an important result three times, in summary, middle, and
    conclusion, and calls title and summary overlap purposeful (pp. 139 to 141). Heard:
    summarize, do not repeat, and watch the three redundancy hotspots (pp. 122, 186).
    Zinsser: no recapitulation. Ruling: repetition across parts is purposeful; within a
    part it is clutter.
12. **Titles.** Alley rejects "short and sweet" and lengthens titles to name the field and
    the distinguishing detail (pp. 110 to 114). Heard wants brief, clear, informative,
    expressive of the story, and allows the assertive sentence title (pp. 79 to 80). The
    two agree on specificity. Ruling: specific over short; a sentence title only where the
    field accepts it.
13. **Technical terms.** Alley: jargon is efficient inside a workplace and alienating
    outside; the author bridges the language gap (pp. 99 to 100). Heard: technical terms
    save length and add clarity for the audience that holds them, and become jargon when a
    simpler word suffices (p. 177). Zinsser: does the word fill a need and convey a picture
    (pp. 41, 45); plain style is needed for peers, not only laymen (p. 161). Ruling: use a
    technical term when the audience holds it, define it once, never lean on it.

## Models the books recommend

Zinsser's scientist-writers to imitate for linear style, absence of jargon, and the constant
relating of an arcane process to something a reader can picture: Primo Levi, Peter Medawar,
Oliver Sacks, Stephen Jay Gould, S. M. Ulam, Paul Davies, Freeman Dyson (p. 159), plus Lewis
Thomas, Loren Eiseley, Rachel Carson, and Darwin's *Voyage of the Beagle*. Alley's models:
Maria Goeppert Mayer, Einstein, Gertrude Elion, Stephen Hawking, Jane Goodall (p. 29), and,
for crisp nonfiction, Richard Rhodes, Laura Hillenbrand, Jon Krakauer (p. 207). Zinsser's
standing instruction: read *The Elements of Style* once a year (p. 36).
