# Structure

Purpose: shape a scientific document so the reader finds the story, meets the parts they expect, and keeps the point you want them to keep.

Lead source: Heard, *The Scientist's Guide to Writing*, Part III, for the journal paper. Supplements: Alley, *The Craft of Scientific Writing*, Lessons 1 and 6 to 9, for titles, introductions, emphasis, proposals, reports, and long documents; Zinsser, *On Writing Well*, chapters 8, 9, 15, and 23, for leads, endings, unity, and the organization of a long piece; Strunk, rule 8, for the paragraph plan of a composition.

## 1. Before the document: audience, purpose, occasion

Ask three questions about the audience before you draft: who they are, why they are reading, and what they know. Who sets the difficulty; why sets the scope and what to emphasize; what sets the order, the definitions, and the background (Alley, p. 4). [firm]

Heard asks the same three in his own words: who is the audience, why will they read it, and what do you want them to get out of it. "the key to every writing form is the same" (Heard, p. 233). [firm]

For a controversial finding, also ask what the audience believes. With a neutral audience, assert and then give evidence; against a strong prior belief, show you understand their position, give the evidence, and assert last (Alley, p. 5). [judgment: Alley concedes it "will not necessarily win over opponents with strong biases"]

Name one primary audience and write the main text for it. Serve secondary audiences through appendices and a glossary rather than by diluting the main text (Alley, p. 5). [firm]

Decide whether the document informs or persuades. Informative writing is measured by efficiency; persuasive writing must also spend words on credibility, which means answering why (Alley, pp. 6-8). [firm] Expect longer paragraphs in the persuasive case, because the why needs explanation (Alley, p. 8).

Let the length allowance set the depth. A journal page limit may allow one sentence of justification where a report or a thesis allows several (Alley, pp. 10-11). [firm]

Reduce the subject before you write. "Nobody can write a book or an article 'about' something" (Zinsser, p. 52), and an unwieldy task drains your enthusiasm. [firm]

Decide the one thought you want to leave with the reader, "not two thoughts, or five" (Zinsser, p. 53). That thought sets the route, the destination, and the tone. [firm]

Settle the unities of pronoun, tense, and mood before drafting (Zinsser, p. 50). See process.md for running that decision as a habit.

Never move text or graphics built for one audience into a document for another without rewriting. "Specific audiences need specific things" (Heard, p. 234). [firm]

## 2. The story

Do not organize around your results. Results are a heterogeneous pile, and the phrase centers the writer: ask "what does my reader need to hear about?" (Heard, p. 57). [firm]

Write a two-sentence mini-summary before drafting: the central question and its answer. It is private, and it settles inclusion, since every candidate detail faces one test, "would including it help tell the story or distract from it?" (Heard, p. 58). [firm]

Heard's four-rung ladder of mini-summaries, all describing one star-formation dataset (Heard, pp. 59-60):

- Very bad: "ALMA provided the most detailed images ever made of the Orion, Eagle, and Carina Nebulae. I explain how ALMA works, and show some of the images." It reports research done, not a question answered.
- Bad: "I present images of star-forming regions in the Orion, Eagle, and Carina nebulae, taken at 8 wavelengths from 0.4 to 10 mm. They show many protostars, some in groups." It names a topic and asks nothing.
- Better, but not good: "I outline what we know about star formation and stellar evolution. I present ALMA images of massive protostars at various stages of stellar evolution." Relevance is attempted; the specific question is still missing.
- Very good: "If massive stars form with gravitational assists from their neighbors, then massive protostars should always appear among other protostars. However, solitary massive protostars are common in ALMA images of the Orion, Eagle, and Carina nebulae." Question and answer are both explicit, and each IMRaD section now has a job.

Watch for symptoms of a missing story (Heard, pp. 60-61). Three that recur: needing more than about five figures and four tables (p. 68) [judgment, field-dependent]; needing a fourth level of heading (p. 67); and a Results section so long that it buries the point (pp. 100-101).

Use hindsight. Report neither everything you did nor the order you did it in, and choose the story the reader should hear "whether or not it's the one they had in mind when they began the work" (Heard, p. 61). [firm]

Brainstorm away from the keyboard. "Do not hope to find your story by sitting down at your keyboard and beginning to type the first line of your Introduction" (Heard, p. 61). [firm]

Work through three forms in order. A wordstack is an unsorted list of candidate ingredients (Heard, p. 62). A concept map has labeled nodes and relations and shows the logical structure (pp. 63-65). An outline is the linear result (pp. 65-68). Keep doubtful wordstack entries, marked, rather than deleting them (pp. 62-63). [judgment]

Linearize before you draft. Despite decades of hypertext, "a linear sequence of ideas is still the most effective way to communicate complex information" (Heard, p. 65). [firm]

Choose the outline's resolution: a story summary, a subhead outline, or a topic-sentence outline (Heard, pp. 65-68).

Try figure shuffling when the analysis is finished: select and order the figures and tables first, and let the question show through the answer (Heard, pp. 68-69). Try writing backwards when the story will not come: conclusion, results, methods, discussion, introduction (Heard, p. 69, after Magnusson).

Omit a result only when it is irrelevant or redundant. Omitting a conflicting result "of course, is unethical" (Heard, p. 69). [firm]

Change the outline only when you can say why the new order tells the story better (Heard, p. 70). [firm]

Sell the story, and say its importance up front, because "few will invest the effort to read through a paper unless its importance is established explicitly right up front" (Heard, p. 71). What matters is the question's importance to the reader, not to you (p. 71). [firm]

Reject the timid framings: "I was interested in studying X", "further studies are required", "no studies have reported". Many conceivable studies pass those tests and interest nobody (Heard, p. 71). [firm] Choose a pitch that fits the audience and the journal; Heard gives five models (p. 72). [judgment]

Make the first sentence carry the reason the document exists. "If it doesn't induce the reader to proceed to the second sentence, your article is dead" (Zinsser, p. 55), and the lead must supply "hard details that tell the reader why the piece was written and why he ought to read it" (Zinsser, p. 56). [firm]

For explanatory passages, use Zinsser's upside-down pyramid. Start at the bottom with the one fact the reader must know, then widen sentence by sentence into significance: what the discovery changes, what it opens, where it applies (Zinsser, p. 150). Readers "will understand the broad implications only if they start with one narrow fact" (p. 150). [firm]

Drive the widening with the reader's next question. Zinsser annotates a news story on brain-wave research by writing the reader's question between paragraphs, from "why is it worth page 1 of the Times?" to "what is the purpose?" (Zinsser, pp. 151-153). After every sentence, ask what the reader wants to know next (Zinsser, p. 268). [firm]

## 3. The canonical paper: IMRaD and the hourglass

Use IMRaD unless a deviation demonstrably serves the reader. It is a finding system: it lets a reader jump to the part they want, and it pulls a linear reader through the story (Heard, p. 75). [firm]

Shape the paper as an hourglass: broad context, narrowing to the central question, narrowest through Methods and Results, broad again at the end of the Discussion. The broad ends sell the story to the largest possible set of readers (Heard, p. 77). [firm]

Keep Results and Discussion separate; Heard calls them "usually (and best) kept separate" (p. 77). [judgment] Merge them only when the gain outweighs the damage to the finding system, and check that a long merged section is not hiding a failure to find the story (pp. 100-101).

Deviate mildly and for one reason only: "deviate from IMRaD not when doing so seems to make the paper easier to write, but only when you are sure it makes the paper easier to read" (Heard, p. 138). [firm]

In a methods-last paper, put enough method into the Introduction's close and into the Results sentences that a reader can understand the data (Heard, pp. 138-139). [firm] Give a review an explicit question in its Introduction and an answer in its conclusion, and include a Methods section if the review is quantitative (Heard, pp. 139-140). [firm]

## 4. Title

Name the field of work and separate this document from every other document in that field. "A document's title is the single most important phrase in a document" (Alley, pp. 110-111), and the test is how it reads in a list of search results. [firm]

Reject "short and sweet". A cryptic title such as "Reducing the Hazards of Operations" tells a reader nothing (Alley, p. 110). Ruling 12 applies: specific beats short, and Alley and Heard agree.

Put the distinguishing detail first, because the heaviest emphasis falls on the first and last details (Alley, p. 112). [firm] Keep secondary details out: "the real estate in titles is too valuable for secondary details" (Alley, p. 112). [firm]

Use a colon to split a long title into an area-of-work half and a distinguishing half (Alley, p. 113). [judgment] Put small function words among technical terms so the relationships show (p. 113). [firm] Name a special document type, such as a proposal or a progress report, in the title itself (p. 113). [firm]

Treat the title as advertisement. It is "the shortest possible summary of your paper" and has seconds to work (Heard, pp. 79-80). Make it brief, clear, informative, and expressive of the story, or at least of the question. [firm]

Do not foreground the method, and do not write "some observations on", which "is so vague as to be useless to a reader" (Heard, p. 80). [firm]

Use an assertive sentence title, which states question and answer together, only where the field accepts it; some readers find it "brash and presumptive" (Heard, p. 80). [judgment, and ruling 12]

Before and after pairs:

- "Effects of Humidity on the Growth of Avalanches" becomes "Effects of Humidity on the Growth of Electron Avalanches in Electrical Gas Discharges" (Alley, p. 111). The field is named, so nobody reads it as snow.
- "Studies on the Electrodeposition of Lead on Copper" becomes "Effects of Rhodamine-B on the Electrodeposition of Lead on Copper" (Alley, pp. 111-112). The distinguishing detail arrives.
- "Effects of Rhodamine-B and Saccharin on the Electric Double Layer During Nickel Electrodeposition on Platinum Studied by AC-Cyclic Voltammetry" becomes "Using AC-Cyclic Voltammetry to Study How Organic Agents Affect the Electrodeposition of Nickel on Platinum" (Alley, p. 112). Secondary details go, and the unique element moves to the front.
- "10 MWe Solar Thermal Electric Central Receiver Barstow Power Pilot Plant Transfer Fluid Conversion Study" becomes "Proposal to Use a New Heat Transfer Fluid in the Solar One Power Plant" (Alley, p. 113). Function words break the noun string and the document type is named.
- "Spectroscopic observations of the Eagle, Orion, and Carina Nebulae" becomes "Protostar distribution and the formation of massive new stars: testing the cluster-assist model" (Heard, p. 80). Method-first becomes question-first.
- The assertive version of the same paper: "Detailed images of protostar neighborhoods do not support the cluster-assist model of massive star formation" (Heard, p. 80).

## 5. Abstract or summary

Know which summary you are writing. An informative summary gives the results and works like a box score; a descriptive summary is a byline, a table of contents in paragraph form, and can be written before the work is done, which is how conference abstracts get submitted. Most real summaries mix the two (Alley, pp. 140-142). [firm taxonomy]

Give the results away. Some readers stop at the summary, and the goal "is not to entice everyone to read to the end of your document" but to inform or persuade the intended audience efficiently (Alley, pp. 139-140, 142-143). [firm]

Put the main result in the Abstract: "an Abstract is not a movie trailer and does not need to avoid plot spoilers" (Heard, p. 83). [firm] Build the Abstract as a mini-IMRaD, question through meaning, typically 200 words or fewer (Heard, pp. 82-83).

Make the summary self-contained, and build it only from repetitions or condensations of the main text. An inertial-navigation summary has to define Kalman filtering because it stands alone (Alley, p. 140). [firm]

Treat overlap between title and summary as purposeful. "Being redundant is a needless repetition of details. The repetition here is purposeful" (Alley, p. 141). Ruling 11 applies: repetition across parts is deliberate, repetition inside one part is clutter.

Draft the descriptive summary early and the informative summary last, by highlighting the most important sentences of a polished draft and then adding transitions and background (Alley, p. 214). [firm]

A chapter abstract is a different object. Write it as an argument précis, not a method-and-results ledger; see house-style.md.

## 6. Introduction

Answer four reader questions before the middle: what exactly the work is, why it is important, what is needed to understand it, and how it will be presented. Not all four need explicit treatment, and not in that order (Alley, p. 114). [firm and judgment together]

Use Swales's three moves: define a territory, establish a niche, occupy the niche (Heard, pp. 84-86). Calibrate the opening breadth to the journal, since *Nature* and *Science* need very broad context while a specialist journal needs only to reach its own readers (Heard, pp. 84-85). [firm]

Avoid three failure modes in the first sentence (Alley, pp. 96-97). The empty beginning asserts importance and adds nothing. The *in medias res* beginning opens on specimens the reader cannot place. The Genesis beginning starts at the beginning of time and promises what the document cannot deliver. [firm]

Alley's turbine-blade pair (Alley, pp. 96-97):

- Before: "The cooling of turbine blades in jet engines is important. This paper focuses on a cooling scheme..."
- After: the same sentence continued with evidence and a citation, "because the combustion gases that flow over the blades are more than 500 °C hotter than the blades' melting temperature [7]."
- In a report, where length allows, Alley adds the blade cost and the doubling of blade life per 25 °C (Alley, pp. 10-11).

Show importance rather than asserting it. Writing that an area's importance "is widely known" persuades nobody, and skipping the justification limits your audience to experts (Alley, pp. 115-117). [firm] If the work is curiosity-driven, raise the questions that made you curious (p. 116). [firm]

Pitch the background just below the audience's elevation of knowledge. You cannot start with Euclid, and Bohr in 1913 would assume Balmer and Coulomb but not Rutherford (Alley, p. 117). [firm]

Make the literature review show the gaps, not just what others have done. Showing that the research is unique is most journals' acceptance criterion (Alley, p. 118). [firm] Describe others' shortcomings respectfully, because one of those researchers will likely review your paper (p. 118). [firm]

In a long document, put the literature review in a table, one row per paper or group, your own work on the last row (Alley, p. 119). [judgment: "I recommend"]

Put only document-wide background in the introduction. Section-specific background belongs in its section, and long document-wide background gets its own section with a descriptive title, not "Background" (Alley, p. 120). [firm]

State the central research question clearly and specifically at the end of the niche move (Heard, p. 86). [firm] Then outline the approach without methods detail: the kind of work, the general form of the data, and how the analysis answers the question (Heard, pp. 86-87). [firm]

End a paper's introduction with the question, the approach, and the main result (ruling 8). Heard: "you're writing a scientific paper, not a mystery novel, and in any case your Abstract has already ruined the suspense" (p. 87). [judgment: Heard names guides on both sides and marks this as his own advice]

End a long document's introduction with a map of the sections instead (Alley, pp. 120-121; ruling 8). Mapping transitions into the middle, conveys your reasoning for the order, and lets you emphasize what each section carries. The longer the document, the more it matters.

Warm the water before you throw readers in. "You must make an immediate effort to orient your readers to the special world they are about to enter" (Zinsser, p. 204), and the lead sentence is the one that decides whether they continue (Zinsser, p. 55). [firm]

## 7. Methods

Do not organize Methods chronologically. "This might be the right way to write a travelogue or an autobiography, but it's the wrong way to write a scientific paper!" (Heard, p. 89). [firm]

Choose one of two schemes: background, then experiments, then analysis; or one subsection per procedurally distinct component (Heard, pp. 89-90). Signal the scheme with subheads, and make the Methods subheads identical to the Results subheads so readers can navigate between them (Heard, pp. 90-91). [firm]

Apply the credibility criterion, plus whatever the venue's reporting checklist requires (ruling 9). Include a detail "if, and only if, it could influence the reader's interpretation of your Results" (Heard, p. 94). [firm]

Run three tests on a candidate detail (Heard, p. 94). Does it establish your qualifications to do the work? Does it establish the plausibility of your approach? Does it establish your sequence of investigative steps? If none of the three, cut it.

The Kolaczan worked example (Heard, pp. 94-95) shows the criterion in operation. In: each parasite came from a different caterpillar in a different gall, and parasitized caterpillars went immediately into 95% ethanol, which forestalls DNA-degradation artifacts. Out: how to recognize a galled plant, how to open a gall, and site locations finer than about 1.5 km. Deliberately omitted: the 4 mL polypropylene vials.

Do not write to the level of detail needed for exact replication. Replication is vanishingly rare, and serving the hypothetical one percent "would reduce clarity and increase reading effort for the vast majority" and would not make replication more likely (Heard, p. 95). [firm; methods papers excepted, p. 91] Heard notes this puts him against the standard guides (p. 91).

Learn your field's detail conventions by reading its literature. Ecology names the statistics software; cell biology rarely does (Heard, p. 95). [firm]

Rewrite, never reuse, your own published Methods wording: "you may own your silverware, but you usually don't own your published words" (Heard, p. 96). Grant-proposal text is exempt, being unpublished. [firm] Heard's paired passages change three things between two papers on one study system: which details appear, the order of the information, and the wording even where the information matches (Heard, p. 97).

Mention a result inside Methods only when the reader needs it to accept the method, and do not discuss it there (Heard, p. 101). His acceptable example: "We used parametric ANOVA to test for differences between treatments. This approach is justified because our data showed no significant deviation from normality of residuals or homoscedasticity" (Heard, p. 101).

## 8. Results

Strip Results to the data the reader needs in order to understand and accept your answer. The section may end up startlingly short (Heard, pp. 99-101). [firm]

Name the pattern; do not dump values. Bare means "ask your reader to figure out what pattern there might be" (Heard, pp. 99-100). [firm]

- Before: "average dry mass was 14.2 ± 1.1 g for fertilized plants and 9.4 ± 2.3 g for unfertilized plants" (Heard, p. 100).
- After: "fertilized plants grew over 50% faster than unfertilized ones (average dry mass 14.2 ± 1.1 g vs. 9.4 ± 2.3 g)" (Heard, p. 100).

Keep the methods firewall. Never introduce methods in the Results, and allow only a brief reminder where the reader needs one: "The separation between Methods and Results is much closer to a firewall" (Heard, p. 101). [firm]

Use one of Heard's three organizing schemes, and treat the first and last paragraphs of the section as power positions (Heard, pp. 101-102). Signal the arrival of the main result in words, with "Finally" or "Most important" (p. 102). [firm]

Put two or three numbers in the text, and use a graphic only for more, because a graphic costs the reader a navigation detour (Heard, p. 103). [firm] Present any set of numbers exactly once, never in text and table and figure (p. 105). [firm]

Winnow. Use the most relevant metric or a data-reduction tool, and park alternatives in a supplement or in one reassuring clause (Heard, p. 106). [firm] Drop redundant numbers, such as counts and percentages on equal denominators, or any two of df, SS, and MS (p. 106). [firm]

Report only significant and necessary precision (Heard, pp. 107-108). [firm] "F = 0.9, P = 0.8" replaces "F = 0.92238674, P = 0.7826" and "carries just as much information and does so more clearly" (p. 108).

Order numbers so the pair you want compared comes first and the supporting pair goes in parentheses (Heard, p. 106). "54% vs. 34% (25/46 vs. 23/67)" emphasizes the percentages; reversing the order emphasizes the counts and sample sizes.

Put the story first and the test second (Heard, pp. 106-107):

- Before: "A χ2-test showed that massive protostars were more likely to have neighbors, with χ2(1) = 4.47, P = 0.03 (54% vs. 34%)".
- After: "Massive protostars were more likely to have neighbors (54% vs. 34%; χ2(1) = 4.47, P = 0.03)".

Name the pattern first, then point at the graphic (Heard, p. 117). "See Table 1 for activation energies in the presence of different catalysts" becomes "Activation energies were lowest on palladium catalysts (Table 1)". Never make one point by sending the reader to more than one graphic; that is data-analytic work "which the writer should have done instead" (p. 117). [firm]

Table and figure design, legends, scales, and captions belong to figures-tables-equations.md.

Repeat the main result across parts on purpose: in the summary, in the middle, and in the conclusion (Alley, p. 139; ruling 11). Readers retain little, so the repetition is structural, not clutter.

## 9. Discussion and conclusion

Treat the Discussion as the place where data becomes knowledge: "Your Discussion's function is to turn data into knowledge" (Heard, p. 120). Claim the strongest interpretation and the broadest importance you can legitimately argue, and calibrate with hedges. [firm]

Answer the research question concisely in the first paragraph, which is a power position (Heard, pp. 121-122). [firm]

Summarize, do not repeat. Brief reminders return the reader's attention to the patterns while you consider what they mean (Heard, p. 122). [firm]

Hedge to match claim strength to evidence, and get the critical questions early at a lab meeting or a conference, because seeing your own limitations is hard (Heard, pp. 120-121; ruling 1). [firm]

Explain surprises. Name the expectation, name its source, and offer at least a plausible explanation, since "surprises are likely to leave your readers unsatisfied unless you can also offer some explanation" (Heard, p. 122). [firm]

Do not argue from anecdote. A specific supporting example is just a data point (Heard, p. 122). [firm]

Write a weaknesses paragraph, and do not overdo it. Early-career writers overdo it, and readers who finish thinking the results mean little "will feel they've wasted their time" (Heard, pp. 122-123). [firm in both directions]

Answer every question the Introduction raised, and raise in the Introduction every major issue the Discussion treats, and only those (Heard, pp. 121, 125). [firm]

Name specific future directions. "more research is necessary" says nothing, while identifying profitable future work emphasizes your paper's importance (Heard, p. 124). [firm]

In a paper, skip a separate Conclusion unless the venue wants one (ruling 10). If you seem to need one, suspect the Discussion needs reorganizing; if you keep it, hold it to four to six sentences (Heard, pp. 124-125). [judgment: "There's no consensus on this question."] End the Discussion with a brief reminder of the most important finding and its significance (p. 125). [firm]

A report, a chapter, or a proposal closes differently (ruling 10). Analyze the results as a whole and give a future perspective: recommendations, the direction of future work, or a return to the big picture (Alley, p. 131). [firm] Introduce no new evidence: "you should not act like Perry Mason and bring in new evidence that unravels the mystery of your project" (Alley, p. 131). [firm] Run it at about 5 to 10 percent of the main text (p. 132), and close the circle, because "You do not have to reach a peak in your conclusion, but you should reach a plateau" (p. 132). [firm]

Escape Roman-numeral III. Phrases such as "In sum, it can be noted that" mark the point where "you are about to repeat in compressed form what you have already said in detail", and readers "notice what you are doing and how bored you are by it" (Zinsser, pp. 64-65). [firm]

When you are ready to stop, stop. "If you have presented all the facts and made the point you want to make, look for the nearest exit" (Zinsser, p. 66). [firm]

Zinsser names three reliable endings: encapsulate and jolt, come full circle, or end on a quotation (Zinsser, pp. 66-67). [judgment, his preferences] Ruling 10 puts the plateau first for chapters, reports, and proposals, so prefer the full circle.

## 10. Back matter and citations as content choices

Apply the three-way placement test (Heard, p. 128). Put material in the body if readers need it to understand the story. Put it in a supplement if some readers will find it useful for other reasons. Leave it unpublished if no plausibly imaginable reader will use it. [firm]

Resist supplement bloat. "Supplements are cheap, but they aren't free", and "if everything is important, then nothing is" (Heard, pp. 127-128). [firm]

Scrutinize supplements more, not less, because reviewers will scrutinize them less. "If you are unconcerned about problems creeping into a supplement, it probably isn't needed in the first place" (Heard, p. 130). [firm] Never use a supplement to slip a weak inference past review (p. 130). [firm]

Check the venue's archiving policy early, because building an archive during analysis is far easier than reconstructing a dataset later (Heard, p. 131). [firm]

Cite for claims readers might question, for unfamiliar methods, and where readers may want more (Heard, p. 134). Do not cite widely accepted facts, easily checked facts, or standard methods (pp. 133-134). [firm, with the caveat that these are judgment calls and conventions vary by field]

Use one to three citations per point. More "carry little additional information but make your text difficult to read" (Heard, p. 134); signal representativeness with "e.g." or cite a review. [firm, review papers excepted]

Choose each citation by one criterion: greatest use to the reader who wants to learn more (Heard, pp. 134-135). [firm] Cite the earliest source for credit and the most recent for current understanding (p. 135). [firm] Prefer accessible sources, journal papers over chapters, theses, abstracts, and reports (p. 135). [firm]

Prefer primary over secondary. Citing a review for a specific result "shunts the work of tracking down the original literature onto your reader, and deprives the original author of a citation" (Heard, p. 135). [firm] If you truly cannot find the original, cite it and say you have not seen it, and let that be rare (p. 135). [firm]

Handle the four odd citations with care: personal communication, personal observation, unpublished manuscript, unpublished data. Minimize them and never rest an important claim on one, because "at best, they are the writer's parenthetical 'no, really!'" (Heard, pp. 135-137). [firm]

If your reference list falls outside 25 to 60 entries, or 6 to 20 in pure mathematics, ask why (Heard, p. 134). [judgment]

Citation formatting, style conversion, and reference-manager mechanics are out of scope here; hand them to a citation tool or skill.

## 11. Emphasis and placement

The O-ring case in three sentences. A 1979 NASA trip report was titled "Visit to Precision Rubber Products Corporation and Parker Seal Company", which named the author's activity and not the seal problem, and the paper trail ended there (Alley, pp. 2-3, 137). The memo also had no conclusion repeating the finding and no future perspective, and its two most important sentences sat in the middles of the two long middle paragraphs (Alley, p. 137). Alley's revised subject line reads "Concern by O-ring Manufacturers about the Secondary Seal Design in the Shuttle's Solid Rocket Boosters" (Alley, p. 137).

Word each sentence so the reader can see why a detail is there. A string of prepositional phrases gives every detail equal weight, while an infinitive phrase or a *because* clause reveals the causal relation and lets readers infer importance (Alley, pp. 138-139). [firm]

- Before: "One of the panels on the north side of the solar receiver will be repainted with Solarcept during the February plant outage."
- After: "Because the February plant outage gave us time to repair the north side of the solar receiver, we repainted the panels with Solarcept, a new paint developed to increase absorptivity." (Alley, p. 138)

Repeat an important result three times, in the summary, the middle, and the conclusion (Alley, p. 139; ruling 11). [firm]

Place important details where text borders white space: titles, headings, the openings and closings of sections, and to a lesser degree of paragraphs. "Where text borders white space is where you achieve emphasis" (Alley, p. 143). [firm] Heard's power positions are the same principle inside the text: the first and last paragraph of a section, and the first and last sentence of a paragraph (Heard, pp. 101-102, 150).

Use a short sentence after a long one, and a short paragraph after a long one, when you want emphasis (Alley, pp. 144-145). [firm]

Do not bury a detail in the middle of a paragraph. "If you want to bury a detail in a report, place that detail in the middle sentence of a middle paragraph" (Alley, p. 138). [firm]

- Before: "Receiver performance includes such receiver characteristics as point-in-time steady state efficiency, average efficiency, start-up time, operation time, operations during cloud transients, panel mechanical supports, and tube leaks."
- After: "In this report, we will evaluate performance by studying the receiver's efficiency, operation cycle, and mechanical wear." (Alley, pp. 145-146)

Limit vertical lists to at most one per two or three pages in a paper, report, or proposal. They disrupt reading like traffic lights and make a document look like an outline, and a table is often better because it gives two or more perspectives (Alley, p. 146). [firm]

Challenge bullets (ruling 6). "The purpose of bullets is to remove hierarchy", removing hierarchy removes emphasis, bullets do not show connections, and bullet lists "are first-draft thinking" (Alley, pp. 147-148). Manuscript prose carries none; numbered lists belong in instructions.

Move a secondary block longer than about a page into an appendix, and keep shorter tangents in the text (Alley, pp. 148-149). Alley names three appendix types: background for a less technical audience, detail too fine for the main story such as the raw data behind plotted curves, and tangential but interesting material. [firm] Give each appendix a beginning, a middle, and an ending, and refer to it in the text before it appears (p. 149). [firm]

Use a glossary as a special appendix for secondary audiences. Alphabetize it, italicize each term at first use, and note once that the italics signal a glossary entry (Alley, pp. 150-151). [firm]

## 12. Headings and depth

Make the paragraph the unit of composition, one paragraph to each topic, and plan the composition as a list of topics before drafting (Strunk, rule 8). Strunk's own worked plans run three to seven topics, and he adds that single sentences should not usually stand as paragraphs, with transitions as the exception. See paragraphs.md for what happens inside a paragraph.

Write descriptive, parallel headings. One-word headings defeat a skimmer hunting for results, and non-parallel headings are like slicing a pie and getting one apple piece and one cherry (Alley, pp. 126-127). [firm]

- Before: "Slurry / Combustion / Pollution / Dry / Combustion / Pollution"
- After: "Coal-Water Slurry / Combustion Efficiency / Combustion Emissions / Dry Pulverized Coal / Combustion Efficiency / Combustion Emissions" (Alley, pp. 126-127)

Never create a single subsection. It is "similar to slicing a pie and ending up with only one piece" and cannot be parallel with anything (Alley, p. 127). [firm]

Test the headings by reading them alone as a table of contents (Alley, p. 128). [firm]

Group items in twos, threes, or fours, because "A list with more than four items taxes the reader's memory" (Alley, p. 122). [firm] Alley splits eight Hawaiian volcano stages into four building and four declining (pp. 122-123).

Stop at three levels of heading. Needing a fourth means the organization is too complex; use a topic-sentence outline instead (Heard, p. 67). [firm]

Choose an organizing strategy for the content and the audience, by trial and error. Alley names chronological, spatial, flow of a variable, classification and division, cause and effect, and comparison and contrast (Alley, pp. 121-125). Most organizational problems come from a logical strategy that is wrong for this audience, not from an illogical one (p. 121). [firm]

Choose a depth that satisfies interest and answers the questions your own details raise. Of Alley's three sample depths the middle one is worst, because it introduces "g/hp-h" without explaining it and is "simultaneously too shallow and too deep" (Alley, p. 129). [firm]

Read paragraph length as a signal: short is 6 lines or fewer, medium is 7 to 11, long is 12 to 15. Keep most paragraphs in the medium band, avoid stacking more than two short ones, and avoid going past 15 lines, with a displayed equation as the exception (Alley, pp. 130-131). [firm] Hold depth consistent across related topics, especially in a comparison (p. 131). [firm]

Prefer paragraphs to subsections where paragraphs can show the organization, especially in a persuasive document (Alley, p. 126). [firm]

## 13. Proposals

Know which of three categories you are in: a solicited industry proposal, an agency research call, or an unsolicited proposal that faces skepticism about the status quo but no direct competition (Alley, pp. 174-175). "Proposals move the money in science and engineering" (p. 174).

Decide go or no-go first, weighing format, politics, and audience. NSF odds are typically one in ten, some calls exist only to satisfy a regulation, and success should be judged over several years of proposals (Alley, pp. 176-179). [firm]

Follow the RFP's format exactly, because the client wants an efficient and fair evaluation and wants to see whether you can hold a line: "if the submitters cannot hold the line on page-length restrictions, then how can the client expect the submitters to hold the line on budget restrictions?" (Alley, p. 177). [firm] Answer unusual RFP questions honestly; of 27 respondents, the only four who advanced were the four who wrote "no experiments today" (pp. 177-178). [firm]

Write for two audiences, technical and management, even when one person reads it: is the science sound, and is the plan feasible in time and money (Alley, p. 180). [firm]

Organize in two parts, the problem or opportunity first and the plan that fills it second, with a front summary that distills both. A committee will read the summary even when only one or two members read the whole proposal (Alley, pp. 182-183). [firm]

Show, do not tell, in the Statement of Problem, example after example (Alley, pp. 183-186). [firm] Build a bridge from a widely recognized problem to your unrecognized one; Alley's turbulence proposal walks from engine efficiency to blade melting temperatures to film cooling to freestream turbulence levels, ending at the absence of a generator that produces more than 20% turbulence with a uniform mean velocity field (pp. 185-186). [firm]

Answer four questions in the plan (Alley, pp. 186-189). What is the proposed solution? Does it make sense technically? Does it make sense managerially, on cost, schedule, environment, safety, and health? Can you carry it out? [firm] Show the effect of the solution at more than one level of depth, not only how much debris is removed but how much safer future missions become (p. 188). [firm]

Use Alley's three winning strategies (pp. 189-196). [judgment, strongly advocated] Stick the beginning, so the reviewer's question shifts from "why turn this down" to how to persuade the committee. Emphasize your assertions, and make the assertions the section headings wherever headings are not prescribed. Show the idea in one graphic, because reviewers generally examine every illustration even when they skip paragraphs.

Prove three things (Heard, pp. 239-240). The question is important to the agency's goals. The question can be answered. You can do the work. [firm] Supply pilot data, which is the best demonstration that you can execute the full-scale study (Heard, p. 240). [firm]

Adapt, do not paste, text from earlier proposals (Alley, p. 179). [firm]

Schimel's story-and-message method of proposal persuasion (*Writing Science*, 2012) sits outside these four books; use a Schimel-based skill, or the book itself, when the user asks for it.

## 14. Reports and theses

Put the primary audience's information in the main text and everything else in appendices, and make the main text stand alone as a document for that audience (Alley, p. 148). [firm]

External company reports are the one place Alley rules out the first person, because "we" is ambiguous between the authors and the company (Alley, p. 55). Ruling 3 otherwise stands; see sentences.md.

Expect the literature review to fill a whole chapter in a thesis, and use the comparison table with your own work on the last row (Alley, pp. 117-119).

Recognize the thesis's three functions and audiences: communicating science, establishing your credentials, and archiving unpublished material (Heard, pp. 240-241). [firm] Settle expectations with your committee before writing, since those functions pull in different directions (pp. 240-241). [firm]

Prefer the papers format over the thesis format. Compartmentalizing lets each part serve its own audience, and it needs the least extra work to publish (Heard, p. 242). [judgment]

"A page a day is a book a year" (Rhodes, quoted in Alley, p. 215), which is a thesis in a semester. Momentum and drafting habits belong to process.md.

## 15. Book chapters and monographs

All four books are thin on this form, so the rules below are assembled from their long-document advice.

Outline a chapter or monograph rigorously and head its sections well, because freedom from IMRaD "can leave the reader adrift in a wandering text" (Heard, pp. 235-236). [firm] Rewrite for the specific audience rather than moving text between audiences unaltered (Heard, p. 234). [firm]

Map the sections at the end of the chapter's introduction, as any long document does (Alley, pp. 120-121; ruling 8). Apply the same heading, depth, emphasis, and appendix rules as in sections 11 and 12 above.

Use a section break as a signpost. It "announces to the reader that you have organized your article in a certain way and that a new phase is about to begin", and it also breaks the writing into manageable chunks, so "panic is staved off" (Zinsser, pp. 271-272, 276-277). [firm]

Hold one point per piece (Zinsser, p. 53), and check the unities at the exit, so that the writer who started the trip is the one ending it (Zinsser, p. 284). [firm]

Ask "What is the piece *really* about?" Fondness for material you went to trouble to gather is not a reason to keep it, and Zinsser calls the discipline required "bordering on masochism" (Zinsser, p. 273). [firm] Leave the surplus showing as an intangible: "Readers should always feel that you know more about your subject than you've put in writing" (pp. 273-274). [firm]

Give only the background the venue requires. "Don't give readers of a magazine piece more information than they require; if you want to tell more, write a book or write for a scholarly journal" (Zinsser, p. 268). [firm]

Let the material tell you where the chapter ends, even against the real chronology of the work; Zinsser was "under no obligation to the actual shape of our trip" (Zinsser, pp. 283-284). [firm]

Set a page-a-day floor and protect momentum across a long manuscript (Alley, p. 215); see process.md.

For the author's own chapter rules, including the argument précis abstract and the chapter-closing conclusion, see house-style.md. Chapter architecture and developmental editing across a whole monograph sit outside this skill.

## 16. Checklist

1. Can you state the central question and its answer in two sentences, and does every section serve them? (Heard, p. 58)
2. Does the title name the field and separate this document from every other one in that field? (Alley, p. 111; Heard, p. 79)
3. Does the abstract or summary give the main result away and stand alone without the text? (Alley, p. 140; Heard, p. 83)
4. Does the first sentence both orient and take a real step, avoiding the empty, *in medias res*, and Genesis openings? (Alley, pp. 94-97)
5. Does the introduction move from territory to niche to question, and end with the main result in a paper or a section map in a long document? (Heard, pp. 84-87; Alley, pp. 120-121)
6. Does the literature review show the gap, and describe others' shortcomings respectfully? (Alley, p. 118)
7. Is every methods detail there because it could change how a reader interprets the results? (Heard, p. 94)
8. Do the Methods subheads match the Results subheads, and is the methods firewall intact? (Heard, pp. 90-91, 101)
9. Does each Results passage name a pattern rather than list values, and does each number appear exactly once? (Heard, pp. 100, 105)
10. Does the Discussion answer the question in its first paragraph, explain surprises, own the weaknesses without overdoing them, and end on the main finding's significance? (Heard, pp. 121-125)
11. Does the ending reach a plateau rather than recapitulate what was already said in detail? (Alley, p. 132; Zinsser, pp. 64-65)
12. Do the headings read as a sensible table of contents, parallel, with no single subsection and no fourth level, and do the important details sit where text borders white space? (Alley, pp. 127-128, 143; Heard, p. 67)
