# Process: getting the draft written

Purpose: the habits that produce a first draft, from reading and starting through momentum, blocks, and outlining.

Lead source: Heard, chapters 3 to 6 and 27. Supplements: Alley, Lesson 10 ("Using Your Writing Time Efficiently"); Zinsser, chapters 1, 2, 8, 10, 21, and 22.

Self-revision is in revision.md. Finding and shaping the story is in structure.md.

## Read as a writer

**Read deliberately and write down what worked and what failed.** A trained ear beats a taped-up
rule list: "The ear you develop from reading (especially with conscious attention to writing) is
worth more than a thousand writing rules" (Heard, p. 20). His exercise: for the next three papers
you read, note one thing you admire and one thing you would improve (Heard, p. 21). Alley makes the
same point from the other end: "How quickly you mature as a writer will depend on how quickly you
mature as a reader" (Alley, p. 219).

**Imitate how something was said, never what was said** (Heard, p. 19). When you paraphrase,
redesign the wording and phrasing independently, because changing every word while keeping the
order and phrasing is still plagiarism (Heard, pp. 19-20). His two Methods passages on one study
system differ in the details selected, the order, and the words (Heard, p. 97). Record the source
of every saved example, even in private notes, or you may later mistake someone else's sentence for
your own (Heard, p. 20).

## Know your own writing behavior

**Decide to be conscious of what you do while you write.** "you can't change what you're doing
unless you know what you're doing" (Heard, p. 23).

**Separate slow-because-hard from slow-because-distracted,** which Heard calls "Flaubert slow" and
"dog-sees-squirrel slow" (Heard, p. 22). The first is the work; the second has a behavioral fix.

**Name which of the Big Six is yours:** avoidance, distraction, feeling stuck, perfectionism, fear
of criticism, reluctance to revise (Heard, pp. 27-29). The diagnosis picks the tactic.

**Keep a daily log in writing, not in your head,** because writing it focuses attention and leaves a
record you can read for patterns (Heard, p. 26). Run a six-minute log on one session when you suspect
interruptions, and rarely, because the log itself interrupts (p. 26) [judgment]. Post a reminder
where you write and refresh it before it goes stale (Heard, p. 25).

## Start

### Make writing the attractive choice

Heard analyzes procrastination with temporal motivation theory, which gives four levers you can
move (Heard, pp. 31-36). In plain words:

- Expectancy, how likely you think you are to succeed: shrink the task, or add a coauthor.
- Value, what finishing is worth to you: make the eventual payoff visible to yourself.
- Discounting, how far a nearby reward outweighs a distant one: remove the competing rewards, so
  turn off the network and delete the email icon.
- Delay, how long until the payoff arrives: reward yourself early and small, and build the habit
  until starting needs no decision.

### Start before the last datum

**Start as soon as you roughly know the story you will tell.** Rewriting two paragraphs later "will
hardly be noticeable among all the other revision you'll be doing", and reviewers demand new
analyses anyway, so the "last" datum is a fiction (Heard, pp. 36-37). Roughly knowing the story
means being able to state a central question and its answer in two sentences (structure.md carries
the story-finding tools):

- Weak: "I present images of star-forming regions in the Orion, Eagle, and Carina nebulae, taken at
  8 wavelengths from 0.4 to 10 mm. They show many protostars, some in groups." A topic, with no
  question (Heard, p. 59).
- Strong: "If massive stars form with gravitational assists from their neighbors, then massive
  protostars should always appear among other protostars. However, solitary massive protostars are
  common in ALMA images of the Orion, Eagle, and Carina nebulae." (Heard, pp. 59-60).

**Do not wait to transcribe a finished mental draft,** because "almost nobody can actually write
like that" (Heard, p. 37) and writing is exploratory: "Putting words on the page does something for
you that won't happen inside your head, no matter how long you wait for it" (Heard, p. 38).

**Do not depend on inspiration.** "Writers dependent on magic always find themselves, sooner or
later, abandoned by it" (Heard, pp. 37-38). Zinsser prescribes a daily schedule instead, and work
on bad days: "Writing is a craft, not an art, and the man who runs away from his craft because he
lacks inspiration is fooling himself" (Zinsser, p. 4).

### Write early, while the science is running

**Draft the Methods while you plan and execute the work.** The details are fresh, and writing them
out exposes design flaws while you can still fix them, so watch for methods that are hard to
explain (Heard, p. 38).

**Build mockup figures and tables from pilot or simulated data before you collect any, labeled
"simulated data only".** It test-drives the design, and it has caught flaws in Heard's own work
(Heard, p. 39). Do not polish early writing: point form and ugly prose keep its cost low, so you
lose nothing when the design changes (Heard, p. 39). (example: ours) A mockup panel plots bloom
onset against sea surface temperature for six simulated sites, captioned "Simulated data only:
layout test, not results."

**Begin at whatever point is easiest.** Heard starts with the acknowledgements: "By the time readers
see your work, they won't know or care where you started" (Heard, pp. 40-41) [judgment]. Alley
sprints through the introduction and rewrites it last (Alley, p. 213) [judgment].

### Settle the unities before you draft

**Fix pronoun, tense, and mood before the first sentence.** Unity "satisfies the readers'
subconscious need for order and reassures them that all is well at the helm" (Zinsser, p. 50). Any
tone is acceptable; mixing two or three is not (Zinsser, p. 50).

**Answer six questions first** (Zinsser, p. 52): in what capacity will you address the reader; what
pronoun and tense; what style; what attitude toward the material; how much will you cover; "What one
point do I want to make?" Zinsser marks the last two as the important ones.

**Reduce the project before you write.** "Every writing project must be reduced before you start to
write" (Zinsser, p. 52), partly for morale, since an unwieldy task drains enthusiasm. Think small,
and drop the search for the last word: "There is no last word... Tolstoy couldn't write a book about
war and peace, or Melville a book about whaling" (Zinsser, p. 52).

**Leave the reader one thought.** "Every successful piece of nonfiction should leave the reader with
one provocative thought that he or she didn't have before" (Zinsser, p. 53). Not two, and not five.
That choice sets route, destination, and tone.

## Keep momentum

**Set a quota, in words produced or hours worked.** "a resolution to write 'enough' is fuzzy and
easily gamed, while a quantitative quota is unambiguous" (Heard, p. 44). Heard is firm that you need
one and leaves the kind to you.

**Schedule the sessions, so the decision is not remade daily** (Heard, p. 44). Writing early helps,
because willpower behaves like a resource that unrelated tasks draw down (Heard, p. 45) [judgment].
Clear your head first by sleep or motion (Alley, p. 205) [judgment].

**Remove the distraction cues, not only the distractions,** because "a conscious decision to resist
distraction works only as long as it remains conscious" (Heard, p. 45).

**Protect a block of one to four hours and kill interruptions inside it.** You need about ten minutes
to reach working speed, and every interruption costs ten minutes again, so do not plan to write all
day (Alley, p. 206).

**Prefer frequent short sessions to rare long ones, and match the task to the session length.** Heard
reports Boice's data favoring daily sessions of 30 to 90 minutes (Heard, pp. 46-48); Alley prefers a
longer block (Alley, p. 206). Both make the session bounded and scheduled.

**Swoop, do not bash.** Swoopers write fast and fix afterward; bashers perfect each sentence before
moving on. True bashers are rare, and most self-described bashers are what Heard calls draggers,
whose premature perfecting is discarded anyway (Heard, pp. 49-51). "Storming the beach means charging
ahead full-speed, although not necessarily in a straight line" (Heard, p. 50). Alley's version is
rabbit or turtle: be a rabbit when stuck, a turtle on a section that fixes the organization (Alley,
pp. 212-213) [judgment]. Set a floor for each sitting, one page for a turtle and three for a
rabbit: "A page a day is a book a year" (Rhodes, quoted in Alley, p. 215).

**Use placeholders and keep moving.** Heard writes `???`, `(???citation???)`, and `(???reorder???)`
(Heard, p. 50); Alley hunts three minutes for a missing word, then drops a placeholder and goes on
(Alley, p. 216). (example: ours) "Warming explained (???how much???) of the variance in bloom onset
(???Chen et al.???), which we revisit in the Discussion."

**End every session and every interruption mid-thought, with a loose thread.** "Let that loose thread
pull you up to speed" (Heard, pp. 48-49). Alley agrees and ends a sitting by writing into the next
section (Alley, p. 215).

**Save each finished draft under its own file name,** so a clean printable version exists if a
supervisor asks for the draft in mid-revision (Alley, p. 215). Under deadline pressure, do not cut
the first draft short: a structure that fails, discovered late, leaves the document in pieces when
the deadline arrives (Alley, p. 216).

### Blocks

**Treat a block as behavior, not as an obstacle.** "A 'block' sounds like an obstacle imposed on you
from outside... But really, writer's block comes from inside the writer" (Heard, p. 51).

**Change behavior deliberately for ten minutes, then go back to the text.** Heard offers nine tactics
and caps each at ten minutes, because past that "you're not taking a break, you're succumbing to
distraction!" (Heard, pp. 51-53). The exit is the same either way: "there is no other way to get past
writer's block than to just write. You're a professional; that's what professionals do" (p. 54).

**Diagnose the stoppage before you treat it.** Alley names three and their fixes (Alley, pp. 216-217):
you cannot find the word, so search three minutes and then use a placeholder; you cannot find the
sentences, which usually means you have not grasped the idea, so skip two lines, keep writing, and
let it simmer; you are hearing critical voices, so drown them with music. "In scientific writing,
there really is no such thing as writer's block" (Alley, p. 217), because the real block is a block
in the science. Alley locates it in the science and Heard in behavior, so check the science first.

## Outline as a habit

**Brainstorm before you draft.** Heard's sequence is a wordstack (an unsorted list of possible
ingredients), then a concept map of how they relate, then a linear outline, because "a linear
sequence of ideas is still the most effective way to communicate complex information" (Heard,
pp. 62-65). Keep doubtful wordstack entries, marked, rather than deleting them (pp. 62-63).

**Pick the outline resolution the job needs,** from three forms of increasing detail: story summary,
subhead outline, topic-sentence outline (Heard, pp. 65-68). Stop at three levels of heading, because
a fourth means the organization is too complex; use a topic-sentence outline instead (Heard, p. 67).

**Keep a working outline for yourself and a clean one for other people.** The working outline carries
subsection headings, notes, and any sentence that occurs to you, written down at once (Alley,
pp. 211-212). Alley's own sample includes an item that reads "What else?" (Alley, p. 211).

**Change the outline only when you can explain why the change tells the story better** (Heard, p. 70).
Zinsser pushes the other way: "Don't ever become the prisoner of a preconceived plan. Writing is no
respecter of blueprints" (Zinsser, p. 53), and nonfiction writers "must wriggle out of III's dread
grip", the composition-class outline (Zinsser, p. 65). For scientific documents, outline with Alley
and Heard; use Zinsser's license only when the material has handed you a better story, then rewrite
the whole for consistency. See structure.md.

## Attitude

**Treat writing as a craft you practice, not a gift you have.** You see other people's product, never
their process (Heard, pp. 11-12). Attending to how you write "will let you write more, write more
easily, and write better" (Heard, p. 13).

**Expect it to be hard.** "If you find that writing is hard, it's because it is hard" (Zinsser, p. 12).
Force the thinking: "Clear thinking becomes clear writing; one can't exist without the other"
(Zinsser, p. 9), and thinking clearly "is a conscious act that writers must force on themselves" (p. 12).

**Expect quality from revision, not from the first draft.** "Revision allows an average writer (such
as me) to craft a quality document" (Alley, p. 230), and success "does not depend on a single burst of
inspiration but the patient building of one sentence upon another" (Alley, p. 214).

**Turn on the enjoyment yourself.** "Writers have to jump-start themselves at the moment of
performance... Nobody is going to do it for you" (Zinsser, p. 245). Fear of writing is learned, and
confidence is part of the craft (Zinsser, ch. 21). Write about what interests you: "Writers who write
interestingly tend to be men and women who keep themselves interested" (Zinsser, p. 247).

**Judge the process, not the finished object.** "If the process is sound, the product will take care
of itself" (Zinsser, p. 256). Allow time, and allow failure: "The right to fail was as liberating as
the right to succeed" (Zinsser, p. 260).

## Writing in English as an additional language

Heard's chapter 27, compact. He marks most of it as reasoned rather than evidence-based.

**Write in English from the start.** Self-translation costs enormous effort, translators who know the
science are scarce, and phrasing and organization carried over from another language produce poor
English text (Heard, pp. 261-262). If you draft in your first language, write the English version
again rather than translating it (Heard, p. 262).

**Switch to English before the topic-sentence outline stage,** because organization from paragraph up
to subsection follows the conventions of the language you write in (Heard, p. 262). Heard knows of no
data on this point.

**Model published English papers at the largest and smallest scales, organization and fine phrasing.**
Do not model whole sentences or paragraphs, and pick a model outside your own narrow subject, because
closer modeling risks plagiarism (Heard, p. 264).

**Learn the trouble spots your first language creates:** articles, countable and noncountable nouns
and plurals, pronouns, word order, how directly or deferentially a claim is framed, and whether your
language puts the burden of clarity on the writer or the reader (Heard, pp. 265-266).

**Take particular care with plagiarism norms, and consult a more experienced English speaker,** since
penalties reach retraction, expulsion, and revocation of tenure (Heard, pp. 266-267).

**Do not rely on journal reviewers for language help.** They are gatekeepers, and over-reliance "risks
their simply recommending rejection" (Heard, p. 268). If you buy editing help, ask whether the language
expert also knows the discipline (pp. 267-268). Volunteer to write early drafts on a cowriting team and
read what your coauthors change (Heard, p. 263).

## Checklist

1. Can you state the central question and its answer in two sentences before drafting?
2. Are pronoun, tense, and mood fixed, and is the one thought the reader should leave with named?
3. Is there a quota and a scheduled block, with distraction cues removed, not just resisted?
4. Did the last session end mid-thought, leaving a loose thread to restart on?
5. Are uncertain words, citations, and orderings marked with placeholders instead of stalling the draft?
6. Is this draft being written in one pass, with polishing deferred to revision?
7. Were the Methods drafted while the work ran, and is every mockup labeled as simulated data?
8. Does a working outline exist, and can you say why each change to it tells the story better?
9. If a block stopped you, did you check whether the science is unfinished before treating it as a writing problem?
10. For a draft in English as an additional language: was English used from the outline stage on, and was it checked against the trouble spots?
