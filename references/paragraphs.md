# Paragraphs

Purpose: build paragraphs that carry one topic each, open on that topic, connect sentence to sentence, and close where the reader can use the result.

Lead source: Strunk (rules 8 and 9) and Heard (ch. 17). Supplements: Alley (Lesson 5 on connecting sentences, Lesson 7 on paragraph length, Lesson 8 on emphasis and lists) and Zinsser (ch. 10 "Paragraphs" and "Mood Changers", ch. 23 on logic as glue).

## One paragraph, one topic

**Give each topic its own paragraph.** The break itself carries information: it signals the reader that a new step in the subject has been reached (Strunk, rule 8). Heard states the same rule for science: a paragraph treats a single idea, and every break marks a new idea, a new angle on it, or the next step in a sequence (Heard, pp. 149-150).

**Divide the subject into topics before drafting a long section.** Strunk's own lists show the habit: a discussion of a novel divides into setting, plot, characters, purpose; an account of an historical event divides into what led up to it, the event itself, and what it led to (Strunk, rule 8).

**Leave a short subject undivided.** A brief description, a brief summary, or a single idea is best written as one paragraph. Check afterward whether subdivision improves it (Strunk, rule 8).

**Write a single-sentence paragraph only as a transition.** As a rule single sentences should not stand as paragraphs; the exception is a sentence of transition that states how two parts of an exposition or argument relate (Strunk, rule 8). A run of such paragraphs stops reading as transition and starts reading as chop (Zinsser, pp. 80-81).

## The topic sentence

**Open the paragraph with the sentence that declares its idea.** Readers then know the paragraph's purpose as they begin it and can hold that purpose to the end (Strunk, rule 9). Heard allows two or three sentences to carry the topic, "but never more," and notes that in scientific writing the topic sentence "is nearly always the first" (Heard, p. 150). [Firm]

**Let a topic sentence road-map a complex idea without explaining it fully.** Its job is to tell the reader what the paragraph will settle, not to settle it in advance (Heard, p. 151).

**End the paragraph in conformity with its beginning.** The last sentence either restates the thought of the topic sentence or states an important consequence of it. Ending on a digression or an unimportant detail is the failure to avoid (Strunk, rule 9).

**Treat the first and last sentences as the paragraph's power positions.** Heard's paragraph chapter builds on them, and Alley gives the reason: emphasis falls where text borders white space, strongly at titles, headings, and section boundaries, and to a lesser degree at the beginnings and endings of paragraphs (Heard, ch. 17, pp. 150-158; Alley, p. 143).

**In narration and description, a concise comprehensive statement can stand in for a topic sentence.** "The breeze served us admirably." and "The campaign opened with a series of reverses." each hold the following details together. Used too often, the device becomes a mannerism (Strunk, rule 9).

**Put the paragraph's relation to what came before into the topic sentence when a word will carry it.** A single word or phrase (again; therefore; for the same reason) often does the work. When more than one sentence of transition is needed, set the transitional sentences apart as their own paragraph (Strunk, rule 9).

## How a paragraph develops its topic sentence

**Pick a development move, not a heap of sentences.** Strunk names six: restate the topic in other forms, define its terms, deny the contrary, give illustrations or specific instances, establish it by proof, or develop it by showing implications and consequences. A long paragraph may run several of these in turn (Strunk, rule 9).

Strunk's two worked analyses show what that looks like sentence by sentence.

Stevenson, *Walking Tours*, nine sentences (Strunk, rule 9): (1) topic sentence, "a walking tour should be gone upon alone"; (2) the meaning made clearer by denial of the contrary; (3) the topic repeated in abridged form and supported by three reasons, the third again clarified by denying the contrary; (4) a fourth reason in two forms; (5) the same reason in another form; (6-7) the same reason in Hazlitt's words; (8) repetition of the quotation in paraphrase; (9) a final statement of the fourth reason, heightened to form a strong conclusion.

Lecky, *The Political Value of History*, six sentences (Strunk, rule 9): (1) topic sentence, that a different conception of history grew up in the eighteenth century; (2) the new conception defined; (3) the definition expanded; (4) the definition explained by contrast; (5) the definition supplemented with a further element; (6) conclusion, an important consequence of the new conception.

Both paragraphs spend most of their length on one claim. That is the model for a Results or Discussion paragraph: one finding, developed, then its consequence.

## Unity: cut or move the strays

**Delete or relocate any sentence that does not develop the declared idea.** A stray leaves readers "a kind of mental whiplash," wondering what they missed (Heard, pp. 151-152). [Firm]

Heard's example (Heard, pp. 151-152). An eight-sentence paragraph explains the forces controlling protostar accretion. Sentence 5, "Electromagnetic forces drive stellar winds.", introduces a third force the topic sentence never promised. Sentence 8, "Very massive stars are the source of elements heavier than iron in our universe.", drifts off the topic altogether. The revision is the same text minus those two sentences: sentence 5 is deleted as a stray thought, and sentence 8 moves back to an earlier paragraph whose job was to justify interest in massive stars. Note the two different fixes. A stray is not always wrong; sometimes it is right somewhere else.

**Test distinctness in both directions.** A paragraph should be unified (one idea), coherent (the sentences fit together), and distinct (its idea is not another paragraph's idea) (Heard, p. 150).

## Choosing an organizational scheme

**Signal the order in the topic sentence, or adopt a standard scheme the reader already uses.** Heard's seven: spatial or temporal; general to specific; specific to general; least to most important; familiar to unfamiliar; simple to complex; certain to uncertain (Heard, pp. 153-154). Each matches a way readers already organize thinking, so the reader spends attention on the content instead of the arrangement. [Firm]

**Open from the familiar end when the audience is mixed.** Begin with what the audience knows before moving to what is new (Alley, p. viii).

## Connecting one sentence to the next

**Begin each new sentence in a way that connects it to the one before.** The connection then lands early, where the reader needs it, instead of at the end (Alley, p. 71). Alley's second claim is worth knowing because it saves work: scientific connections shift naturally among what, who, when, where, how, and why, so varying the connection varies the opener, the structure, and the length for free (Alley, pp. 71-73). [Firm]

Alley's Mount St. Helens pair (Alley, p. 72):

- Before: "Mount St. Helens erupted on May 18, 1980. A cloud of hot rock and gas surged northward from its collapsing slope."
- After: "Mount St. Helens erupted on May 18, 1980. Its slope collapsing, the mountain emitted a cloud of hot rock and gas."

The link (the collapsing slope) moves from the end of the second sentence to its beginning, and a participial opener breaks the subject-noun cadence.

A second Alley pair shows the same move across three sentences (Alley, pp. 50, 57):

- Before: "A new process . . . is presented. Flow tube experiments . . . are discussed. The percentage decrease . . . is revealed."
- After: "This paper presents a new process for eliminating nitrogen oxides from diesel exhaust engines. To test this process, we performed experiments in flow tubes. These experiments revealed a 99 percent decrease in nitrogen oxide emissions."

Each revised sentence opens on the item the previous sentence ended with, so the paragraph reads as one chain instead of three announcements.

**Keep an assortment of linking adverbs in play, in three categories.** Continuing: also, in addition, moreover, furthermore, therefore, as a result, for that reason, consequently. Pausing: in other words, put another way, in essence, in effect, for example, for instance, likewise, similarly. Contrasting: however, on the other hand, in contrast, conversely, still, nonetheless, otherwise, alternatively (Alley, Table 5-1, p. 75). Openers carry heavy emphasis, so repeating one inside a paragraph or a page distracts.

**Own the five basic openers and use them freely:** subject noun, prepositional phrase, adverb, infinitive phrase, dependent clause (Alley, pp. 75-79). Prepositional phrases can map a whole section ("Before the eruption", "During the eruption", "After the eruption"). The infinitive phrase answers why and carries a measured first person without emphasizing it. A dependent clause warns the reader early that the sentence will be long.

**Use the five sophisticated openers sparingly:** participial phrase as modifier, gerundial phrase, dependent clause as subject, correlative conjunction, comparative correlative (Alley, pp. 79-82). They depart from the subject-verb cadence, so "you do not want to use these spices too often." Keep a participial opener short and make sure the noun that follows is what it modifies. The dependent-clause subject and the comparative correlative suit a paragraph's last sentence, where you want end emphasis. [Judgment]

**Use the five special-occasion openers with the risk in mind:** question, appositive, adjective phrase, introductory series, direct object (Alley, pp. 82-84). A question works mid-paragraph once details are on the table, and must be answered in the same paragraph. Do not open a document with one, and do not end one with one; readers expect closure (Alley, p. 83). [Firm]

**Intersperse simple sentence structures among complex ones.** A simple sentence positions the reader to absorb the complex idea that follows (Alley, p. 79; Heard, p. 170).

**Notice when you tire of your own draft.** The place where you need a break is usually the place where the openers went stagnant (Alley, p. 85). [Judgment]

## Relational devices, and their calibration

**Fit the pieces together for the reader.** Heard's three devices are parallelism, repetition, and transitional expressions. Leaving the reader to infer the links "is really a job that belongs to you as the writer" (Heard, p. 154). [Firm]

Heard's volcano pair (Heard, p. 155). Both versions carry exactly the same information.

- Without: "Lava from the 1998 eruption was richer in metals than that from the 1983 or 1977 ones. The 1998 lava was denser and less CO2-rich. Ejecta volumes were very similar. We cannot reject the hypothesis that eruptions are driven by periodic filling of a single magma chamber."
- With: the same four sentences plus *furthermore*, *Despite these differences*, and *As a result*.

Heard's verdict: "The two versions contain exactly the same information, but the second is much easier to understand" (Heard, p. 155).

**Calibrate parallelism and repetition rather than swinging between them.** Beginners overuse both, are told to vary, then overcorrect. "A little variety keeps your reader awake, but too much disrupts coherence" (Heard, p. 155). [Judgment]

**Signal a change of direction at the start of the sentence, not in the middle.** One short pivot word placed first does the work of a long summarizing clause: "Yet he decided to go" replaces "Despite the fact that all these dangers had been pointed out to him, he decided to go" (Zinsser, pp. 74-75). Zinsser's mood changers: but, yet, however, nevertheless, still, instead, thus, therefore, meanwhile, now, later, today, subsequently (Zinsser, p. 74).

## Connecting one paragraph to the next

**Reject the claim that paragraphs are independent.** A new paragraph "should take the train onto a new track—but a new track that's part of the same overall journey" (Heard, p. 156). [Firm] This is a deliberate departure from common advice (Heard, p. 156).

**Load the last sentence of each paragraph.** Zinsser treats it as the springboard into the next paragraph, and asks for an extra twist of surprise there (Zinsser, p. 56). In science that twist is usually the finding's consequence, not a summary of the sentences above it.

**After every sentence, ask what the reader wants to know next.** Writing is linear and sequential, and "logic is the glue that holds it together"; tension has to hold from sentence to sentence, paragraph to paragraph, and section to section, so that "every step should seem inevitable" (Zinsser, pp. 265-266, 268).

**Do not break a long paragraph unless you can divide its topic.** A break that does not correspond to a new idea makes a false promise (Heard, p. 156).

**Fix an overstrong dependency by naming both halves of the link.** A paragraph that opens on a bare demonstrative forces the reader back into the previous paragraph (Heard, pp. 156-157).

- Before: "Despite this, it remains clear that . . ." and "For example, Devonian trilobites . . ."
- After: "Despite the difference between replicate experiments, it remains clear that . . ." and "Devonian trilobites provide a good example of a declining lineage . . ."

**Rebuild a weak paragraph by carpentry before you rewrite it.** Zinsser annotates a ten-sentence draft paragraph sentence by sentence, then prints the revision (Zinsser, pp. 86-87). His notes are the moves themselves: put the framing verb first to set the tone; start a contrast with "But", because the contrast has to come first; turn one sentence into a question to break a soporific rhythm; kill a sentence that repeats the one before it, or give it a specific detail; reverse a sentence so the reader knows the time frame changed; reshape the ending so the last word is the one that stays in the reader's ear; break the long final sentence into shorter units. His summary: "They're mainly matters of carpentry: altering the sequence, tightening the flow, sharpening the point" (Zinsser, p. 87).

**Keep paragraph order consistent between paired sections.** Methods and Results, Results and Discussion, Introduction and Discussion: when the second section follows the first section's order, it exploits expectations the reader already holds (Heard, p. 157). [Firm]

## Paragraph length

**Treat Alley's bands as advisory signals, not as a gate.** Short is 6 lines or fewer, medium 7 to 11, long 12 to 15. Aim for variety with most paragraphs in the medium range. Avoid stacking more than two short paragraphs, which reads as a lack of depth. Avoid paragraphs over 15 lines, which intimidate. The stated exception is a paragraph containing a displayed equation (Alley, pp. 130-131). Expect longer paragraphs in persuasive writing, because the *why* takes explanation (Alley, p. 8).

**Read a violated band as a question about the topic, not as an instruction to cut.** A 20-line paragraph usually holds two topics; a run of 4-line paragraphs usually holds one topic chopped into pieces.

**Hold depth steady across related topics, especially in comparisons.** Two compared things described at different depths read as a judgment you did not make (Alley, p. 131).

**Keep paragraphs short, but do not chop them.** "Writing is visual—it catches the eye before it has a chance to catch the brain," so short paragraphs invite the reader in. Verbless midget paragraphs are "disruptive and condescending." Good writers "think in paragraph units, not in sentence units. Each paragraph has its own integrity of content and structure" (Zinsser, pp. 80-81). [Firm, with the opposite warning attached]

Zinsser's example (Zinsser, p. 81). A wire-service report broke a story into four paragraphs, two of them verbless fragments ("Only friends, family and colleagues in stunned sorrow."). His revision runs the same sentences into one paragraph and restores the fragments to sentences: "no explanation—only friends, family and colleagues in stunned sorrow. He also left behind a life story that . . ." Three sentences become one logical unit.

## Emphasis inside and between paragraphs

**Follow a long paragraph with a short one when you want the short one to land.** The same trick works at sentence scale (Alley, pp. 144-145).

**Do not bury a result in the middle.** "If you want to bury a detail in a report, place that detail in the middle sentence of a middle paragraph" (Alley, p. 138). Move the detail to a paragraph's first or last sentence, or give it a paragraph.

**Do not bury a list mid-paragraph.** Group the items and move the group to an emphatic position (Alley, pp. 145-146).

- Before: "Receiver performance includes such receiver characteristics as point-in-time steady state efficiency, average efficiency, start-up time, operation time, operations during cloud transients, panel mechanical supports, and tube leaks."
- After: "In this report, we will evaluate performance by studying the receiver's efficiency, operation cycle, and mechanical wear."

Seven flat items become three grouped ones in a position where the reader can weigh them. Alley's supporting rule: a list with more than four items taxes the reader's memory, so group items into twos, threes, or fours (Alley, p. 122).

**Limit vertical lists to at most one per two or three pages of a paper, report, or proposal.** They interrupt reading like traffic lights and make the document look like an outline. A table is often the better choice because it gives two or more perspectives instead of one (Alley, p. 146).

**Use no bullets in manuscript prose (ruling 6).** Alley's three reasons: the purpose of bullets is to remove hierarchy, and removing hierarchy removes emphasis; bullets do not show connections; and bullet lists "are first-draft thinking" (Alley, pp. 147-148). Numbered lists belong in instructions. Where the connections matter, write the paragraph.

**Match the form to how the reader reads.** A list reader reads one step, turns away, performs it, and has to find the place again, which is why instructions use numbered vertical lists. An argument reader expects strong links between claims, which is why persuasive documents use full paragraphs (Alley, pp. 7-8).

**Prefer paragraphs to subsections when a paragraph can show the organization**, especially in persuasive documents (Alley, p. 126). For headings, see structure.md.

## Checklist

Run these on any draft paragraph or run of paragraphs.

1. Can you state this paragraph's one idea in a single sentence, and is that sentence at or near the paragraph's start? (Strunk, rules 8 and 9; Heard, p. 150)
2. Does every sentence develop that idea, or is one a stray to delete or move elsewhere? (Heard, pp. 151-152)
3. Does the last sentence restate the topic or state its consequence, rather than trailing off on a minor detail? (Strunk, rule 9)
4. Does each sentence open in a way that connects it to the sentence before, and do the openers vary? (Alley, pp. 71-75)
5. Are the links between sentences written out, through transitional expressions, parallelism, or repetition, rather than left for the reader to infer? (Heard, pp. 154-155)
6. Does each paragraph's opening name both halves of its link to the previous one, with no bare "this" or "despite this"? (Heard, pp. 156-157)
7. Is the run of paragraph lengths varied, with most in the medium band and no long stack of short ones? (Alley, pp. 130-131; Zinsser, pp. 80-81)
8. Is every important result in a first or last sentence, not in the middle sentence of a middle paragraph? (Alley, pp. 138, 143)
9. Are mid-paragraph lists grouped into twos, threes, or fours and moved to an emphatic position, with no bullets in prose? (Alley, pp. 122, 145-148; ruling 6)
10. In paired sections, Methods and Results or Results and Discussion, do the paragraphs run in the same order? (Heard, p. 157)
