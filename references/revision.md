# Revision: the order of operations for a self-revision pass

Purpose: how to revise your own draft, from the first wait through the rounds to the final proofread.

Lead source: Heard, chapter 21, with chapter 20's process rule that expression is cut in revision, not in drafting. Supplements: Alley, Lesson 10 (revising and finishing); Zinsser, chapters 2, 3, 4, and 10.

Friendly review, formal review, and responses to reviewers are in review-and-coauthors.md. Drafting habits are in process.md.

## When not to revise

**Do not revise while you draft.** Cut content before and during writing, and cut expression only
in revision, because hunting economy in the first draft costs momentum (Heard, p. 183). [firm]

**Do not revise the moment you finish.** Put the draft away for about a week: "Your draft won't
change in that week, but your ability to look at it critically will" (Heard, p. 194). Alley's
minimum is a full day and a night's sleep, with new ideas written down but the draft unopened
(Alley, pp. 219-220). The two books differ on the interval: take the week when the schedule allows
and the overnight gap when it does not. Do not allow months, which saps momentum and forces you to
update the science (Alley, pp. 219-220).

**Self-revise thoroughly before anyone else reads it,** because "the kindness and patience of your
reviewers are not inexhaustible resources" (Heard, p. 193).

## Cut before you polish

Content comes first "because you will nearly always remove material at this step", and effort spent
polishing text you will delete is wasted (Heard, pp. 200-201). That reason sets the order of every
round below.

**Delete material that does not belong, however much it cost you.** Quiller-Couch's "murder your
darlings" applies to content, not only to style; Heard deleted three days' work and a multi-page
appendix from one paper (Heard, pp. 195-196). He treats excised material as evidence of successful
revision, not of unsuccessful writing (Heard, p. 202).

**Keep a cuts file.** Heard moves deleted passages into a file he calls `cuts_to_maybe_restore`
rather than erasing them, which makes the cut cheap to make (Heard, pp. 195-196).

**Ask what the piece is really about, not just what it is about.** "Fondness for material you've
gone to a lot of trouble to gather isn't a good enough reason to include it if it's not central to
the story you've chosen to tell" (Zinsser, p. 273).

**Throw away the warm-up paragraphs.** Editors routinely discard the first three or four and start
"with the paragraph where the writer begins to sound like himself or herself" (Zinsser, p. 21).

**Expect a large cut.** "Most first drafts can be cut by 50 percent without losing any information
or losing the author's voice" (Zinsser, p. 17). Heard's target is smaller and marked as judgment:
at least 20 percent off the first draft (Heard, p. 201). Treat 20 percent as the floor for a
scientific manuscript and Zinsser's 50 percent as the reminder that more is usually available.

**Run four questions on any passage you are reluctant to cut** (Zinsser, p. 17): "Is every word
doing new work? Can any thought be expressed with more economy? Is anything pompous or pretentious
or faddish? Are you hanging on to something useless just because you think it's beautiful?"

Heard's worked example cuts an abstract from 394 to 276 words, 30 percent shorter (Heard,
pp. 186-189). One representative pair:

- Before: "Here, we present the first study, to our knowledge, showing cryptic genetic
  differentiation in a candidate weed biocontrol agent prior to release"
- After: "We demonstrate, for the first time, cryptic genetic differentiation in a candidate agent
  prior to its release"

His framing of the draft matters as much as the cut: it is "a typical example of a good writer
working at her craft", one who "chose momentum over polish in the first draft" (Heard, p. 189).

## Simulate the reader

**Read what the words say, not what you meant.** Heard calls this "getting out of your own head,
and into the reader's" and names it "probably the single most important piece of the writing craft"
(Heard, p. 196). His gloss: "communicating telepathically with your own mind isn't an impressive
trick" (Heard, p. 196). Box 21.1 grounds it in theory of mind: set aside the knowledge the reader
does not have (Heard, pp. 196-197).

**Break your own familiarity with the text.** Memory is context-keyed, so changing the context
weakens your recall of what you meant (Heard, pp. 197-199, a menu) [judgment]. Read in a new
place, read at your worst time of day, change the font or print it out, read aloud in a flat voice,
and keep a "Be the Reader" sign where it moves and stays visible. Alley's version is the same move:
change the look of the document, hard copy instead of screen, library instead of office (Alley,
p. 219).

**Hunt the four familiarity problems** (Heard, pp. 199-200): unclear pronoun antecedents, topic
sentences that do not match their paragraphs, missing transitions, and knowledge you assumed. The
trouble spots are the abstract, the ends of the introduction, and the methods.

## The rounds, in order

Revise in single-purpose rounds, because "most of us greatly overestimate our multitasking ability",
and reading for logical flow makes the mind skip spelling and wordiness (Heard, pp. 200-201). Heard
prescribes at least five rounds with content first; the ladder below applies that rule to the layers
this skill separates. Run it top to bottom, and never polish what a later round may delete.

1. Content. Check that the story promised in the Introduction is the story delivered in the
   Discussion: read the Introduction's first and last sentences, then jump to the Discussion's last
   paragraph (Heard, p. 201). Make the cuts here. See structure.md. (example: ours) The
   Introduction promises a test of whether warming shifts bloom onset, and the Discussion closes on
   sensor calibration; one of the two has to change.
2. Structure. Section order, headings, what occupies each power position, what moves to a
   supplement. See structure.md.
3. Paragraphs. One idea each, topic sentences that match the paragraph, transitions present. See
   paragraphs.md. What this round does (Heard, pp. 151-152): in an eight-sentence paragraph on
   protostar accretion it deletes the stray "Electromagnetic forces drive stellar winds." and moves
   "Very massive stars are the source of elements heavier than iron in our universe." to an earlier
   paragraph.
4. Sentences. Length and load, actor beside action, voice, tense, ambiguity, parallel form. See
   sentences.md. (example, Heard, p. 171) "We developed a gibbon leucocyte cytoplasm fractionation
   protocol." becomes "We developed a protocol for fractionating cytoplasm from gibbon leucocytes."
5. Words. Clutter, hedges, intensifiers, nominalizations, jargon, numbers. See words.md. The kind
   of change this round makes (Heard, p. 184): "(Observation) could reasonably be assumed to
   possibly occur by chance." becomes "The evidence that (observation) was nonrandom was modest
   (P = 0.06)." Zinsser's inflations go in the same round: "due to the fact that" becomes
   "because" (Zinsser, p. 16).
6. Citations. Heard runs a round that does nothing but match in-text citations against the
   reference list (Heard, p. 202).
7. Personal tics. Heard runs another to hunt his own parentheses; keep a growing list of yours
   (Heard, p. 202).

Alley's working conditions for these rounds:

- Do the structural work early and the smoothing late, his "pick and the rake". You cannot afford
  to rebuild the structure in the last passes (Alley, p. 223).
- Sleep between passes, and make one pass a day, because "it is easy to go stir-crazy and start
  changing things for no reason" (Alley, p. 220).
- Work through large chunks per sitting, and eventually read the whole document in one sitting,
  where gaps in logic and faults in structure become visible (Alley, p. 219).
- Read the most important sections aloud (Alley, p. 220).
- Revise until you are nitpicking over a word or a comma (Alley, p. 220).

A content round can rewrite the title. Alley's case (Alley, pp. 208, 217): "Measurements of Surface
Roughness on Additively Manufactured Cooling Channels" became "Effect of Build Direction on Surface
Roughness of Additively Manufactured Cooling Channels", once revision showed the study had tested
one variable's effect, with the measurement a secondary detail.

## Zinsser's rewriting method

**Rewrite by reshaping what you have, not by drafting again.** "Rewriting is the essence of writing
well: it's where the game is won or lost" (Zinsser, p. 84). It means "reshaping and tightening and
refining the raw material you wrote on your first try" (Zinsser, p. 85).

**Assume a newly written sentence is faulty, and name the fault.** Zinsser's list: "It's not clear.
It's not logical. It's verbose. It's klunky. It's pretentious. It's boring." And: "It's full of
clutter. It's full of clichés. It lacks rhythm. It can be read in several different ways. It doesn't
lead out of the previous sentence" (Zinsser, pp. 84-85).

**Ask two questions on every passage that will not come clear:** "What am I trying to say?" and then
"Have I said it? Is it clear to someone encountering the subject for the first time?" (Zinsser,
p. 12). Writers "surprisingly often" cannot answer the first.

**Try the quickest fix first, which is deleting the troublesome element.** "It was trying to do an
unnecessary job all along" (Zinsser, p. 80).

**Bracket rather than cross out when you edit someone else's draft,** which leaves the sentence
intact for the writer to analyze and decide (Zinsser, pp. 16-17). What the brackets go around: the
preposition stuck to a verb ("order up"), the adverb carrying the verb's meaning ("smile happily"),
the adjective stating a known fact ("tall skyscraper"), the little qualifiers, the meaningless
phrase, and sometimes a whole sentence that repeats the one before it (Zinsser, pp. 16-17).

**Read the whole piece aloud with the connecting links in mind.** It exposes "a dismaying number of
places where you lost the reader, or confused the reader, or failed to tell him the one fact he
needed to know, or told him the same thing twice" (Zinsser, pp. 87-88). For the antecedent failure
this catches, the Othello and Iago pair, see sentences.md.

**Expect several passes even on text that already looks finished.** The marked-up pages Zinsser
reproduces "look like a first draft", but "they had already been rewritten and retyped... four or
five times", and reading aloud still found clutter to cut (Zinsser, p. 11). His own worked
demonstration is mostly "matters of carpentry: altering the sequence, tightening the flow,
sharpening the point" (Zinsser, pp. 86-87).

## Criticism

**Solicit criticism, and be willing to act on it** (Alley, pp. 220-221).

**Treat an agitated critic as a frustrated reader,** someone who wants to understand and cannot
(Alley, pp. 220-221). Weigh each criticism; if one seems odd, mull it over, because a deeper problem
may sit under it that the critic could not name.

**Do not seek too many critics.** The Department of Energy put a five-year plan in front of 200
reviewers, and the report was scrapped after years of haggling (Alley, p. 221).

Friendly review, formal review, and the response to reviewers are in review-and-coauthors.md.

## Finishing

**Switch from improving to correcting.** Finishing is a different mindset from revising, and Alley
calls it "a hustle stage" (Alley, pp. 224, 230).

**Do not let up on the final proofread.** A missing word in a heading or a first sentence unsettles
readers and undercuts the work (Alley, p. 224). Typos do the most damage in titles, headings,
summaries, and first sentences (Alley, p. 263).

**Use a spell-checker as a supplement, never a replacement.** It passes real-word errors (*then*
for *than*) and missing words ("in chamber") (Alley, p. 225).

## Stopping

**Stop at good, not at perfect.** "Strive for perfection but be content with success" (Alley,
p. 224). Connell knew a story was finished when he was removing commas and then putting them back in
the same places (Alley, pp. 223-224).

**Remember what self-revision is for.** "Your job in self-revision is not to achieve absolute
perfection by yourself, but rather to make it as easy as possible for friendly and formal reviewers
to help you" (Heard, pp. 202-203). Then stop: "When you're ready to stop, stop" (Zinsser, p. 66).

## Checklist

1. Has the draft rested, a week if the schedule allows and at least a night if it does not?
2. Is the story promised in the Introduction the story the Discussion delivers?
3. Did the content round run first, with the cuts made before anything was polished?
4. Is the draft at least 20 percent shorter than the first draft, with every cut parked in a cuts file?
5. Was each round single-purpose, and was structural work done before smoothing?
6. Was the draft read in an unfamiliar place, time, or format, as a reader would meet it?
7. Are antecedents, topic sentences, transitions, and assumed knowledge checked in the abstract, the end of the introduction, and the methods?
8. Did a citation round match every in-text citation against the reference list?
9. Did a separate pass hunt your own known tics?
10. Were the important sections read aloud, with the links between sentences in mind?
11. Did the last pass correct rather than improve, with titles, headings, and first sentences proofread?
12. Are you nitpicking over a comma? If so, stop.
