# Sentences

Purpose: write scientific sentences that carry one idea, put the actor next to the action, keep the new information at the end, and punctuate so the meaning cannot slip.

Lead source: Alley (Lessons 2, 3, 4) and Heard (ch. 18). Baseline: Strunk (rules 3 to 7, 10, 11, 14 to 18). Supplement: Zinsser (ch. 10 on verbs, punctuation, mood changers, that and which; ch. 23 on one thought per sentence).

## 1. Length and load

**Keep the average sentence length in the teens.** Alley's rule is stated for the document, not for every sentence: "For clear writing, your sentence length in a document should average in the teens" (Alley, p. 20). His negative model is a Bohr passage that averages almost 40 words and is "murky, almost inaccessible" (Alley, p. 20). [Firm as a target]

Alley also gives a readability dial: aim for a Gunning Fog reading level of 10 to 13, because too low is choppy and too high pulls attention onto how the document is written rather than what it says (Alley, pp. 28-29). Treat the direction as sound and the number as advisory only. This file mentions it once and does not use it again.

**Write one idea per sentence.** Alley takes the rule from Bernstein, "One idea, one sentence" (Alley, p. 26). Zinsser gives the reason: "Readers can process only one idea at a time, and they do it in linear sequence. Much of the trouble that writers get into comes from trying to make one sentence do too much work" (Zinsser, pp. 266-267). [Firm in both]

Alley's worked case (Alley, pp. 25-27). The original is a 61-word sentence on the electrical breakdown of nitrogen, carrying eleven stacked prepositional phrases. His diagnosis: "In this sentence, the prepositional phrases are stacked like boxcars on a train. They provide no momentum, only friction" (Alley, p. 26). Two revisions follow.

- First revision: "At relatively high pressures and typical electrode gap distances, the electrical breakdown of nitrogen was studied in uniform fields."
- Second revision: "This study tests the electrical breakdown of nitrogen in uniform fields. For these tests, the electrode gap distances were typical, while the pressures were relatively high."

Eleven prepositional phrases become three. The second revision adds words rather than cutting them, because the distinguishing detail deserved its own sentence.

**Do not stack prepositional phrases in front of or behind the main clause.** A string of them gives every detail the same weight and tells the reader nothing about why any detail is there (Alley, pp. 25-26, 138).

**Give an early clue when a sentence will be long.** Open with a dependent clause ("Although . . .") so the reader expects 20 to 30 words and holds the opening in mind (Alley, p. 26). A 25-word sentence that signals its length is easier than an 18-word sentence that ambushes the reader.

**Avoid stack-loading, which buries the topic at the end.** "Computers are very good at handling stacks, while humans are not" (Heard, pp. 171-172). Heard's example, from a thesis (Heard, pp. 171-172):

- Before: "Using data obtained from our genetic structure analyses, we selected markers that showed polymorphism in at least 50% of the populations to examine allele frequencies along environmental gradients to determine whether environment influenced genetic composition."
- After: "If environment influences genetic composition, allele frequencies should change along environmental gradients. We tested this hypothesis using data from our genetic structure analyses, selecting markers that showed polymorphism in at least 50% of studied populations."

The buried topic (the hypothesis) moves to the front, the sentence splits in two, and the second sentence opens on what the first one ended with.

**Drill a troublesome sentence down to its subject, verb, and object, fix that core, then add back only what is necessary.** Agreement errors and lost actors hide in "thickets of modifiers" (Heard, pp. 167-168). [Firm]

**Vary the length, and let a short sentence carry the punch.** "There is no minimum length for a sentence that's acceptable in the eyes of God" (Zinsser, p. 72). Alley makes the same point as an emphasis device: a short sentence after a long one, like the Warren Commission's delayed "His name was Lee Harvey Oswald," lands hard (Alley, pp. 144-145).

**Interrupt a block of long complex sentences with short simple ones.** The cadence improves and the simple sentence positions the reader to absorb the complex idea next to it (Heard, p. 170; Alley, p. 79). [Judgment in Heard]

**Treat complex sentences with suspicion and simplify where you can** (Heard, p. 172).

## 2. Word order and emphasis

**Put the main verb early; do not park it before the period.** The verb is the engine, and delaying it is "akin to hitting the accelerator of a car and then slamming on the brakes" (Alley, pp. 50-51). Alley's fix is either to move the verb up or to flip the sentence so a participle opens it.

- Before: "In this study, the care given by a hospital and the medical outcomes of patients are compared. The effects of exogenous factors . . . are accounted for. Also, the opinions . . . are considered."
- After: "This study compares the care given by a hospital and the medical outcomes of patients. Accounted for in the study are the effects of exogenous factors . . . Also considered are the opinions . . ." (Alley, pp. 50-51)

**Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end.** The position of prominence belongs to the new element, the logical predicate (Strunk, rule 18).

- Before: "This steel is principally used for making razors, because of its hardness."
- After: "Because of its hardness, this steel is principally used in making razors." (Strunk, rule 18)

The beginning is the other prominent position: any element other than the subject becomes emphatic when placed first ("Deceit or treachery he could never forgive"). A subject in first position is rarely emphatic by position alone (Strunk, rule 18). The same principle applies to the sentences of a paragraph and the paragraphs of a document (Strunk, rule 18; see paragraphs.md).

**Keep related words together.** Word position is the main signal of relationship, so bring related words together and keep unrelated ones apart (Strunk, rule 16).

- Subject and principal verb: "Wordsworth, in the fifth book of *The Excursion*, gives a minute description of this church." becomes "In the fifth book of *The Excursion*, Wordsworth gives a minute description of this church."
- Relative pronoun next to its antecedent: "He wrote three articles about his adventures in Spain, which were published in *Harper's Magazine*." becomes "He published in *Harper's Magazine* three articles about his adventures in Spain."
- Modifier next to what it modifies: "He only found two mistakes." becomes "He found only two mistakes." (Strunk, rule 16)

Alley's version of the same fix: "The proposed schedule is discussed below for the next four years." becomes "The proposed schedule for the next four years is discussed below" (Alley, pp. 40-41).

**Place "only" deliberately.** Five positions in a six-word sentence give five meanings, because *only* is sometimes an adjective and sometimes an adverb (Alley, pp. 41-42): "Only I sighted the cheetah yesterday" (nobody else did), "I only sighted the cheetah yesterday" (I did not photograph it), "I sighted only the cheetah yesterday" (no other animal), "I sighted the only cheetah yesterday" (there was one), "I sighted the cheetah only yesterday" (as recently as yesterday).

**Make a participial phrase at the beginning of a sentence refer to the grammatical subject.** Sentences that break this rule are often ludicrous: "Being in a dilapidated condition, I was able to buy the house very cheap" (Strunk, rule 7). The same rule covers opening appositives, adjectives, and adjective phrases: "Young and inexperienced, the task seemed easy to me" becomes "Young and inexperienced, I thought the task easy" (Strunk, rule 7).

Alley's version (Alley, p. 238): "Shooting at speeds of 100 mph, the technicians tested the solar mirrors for hailstone damage." becomes "To test for mirror damage, the technicians fired hailstones at 100 mph onto the solar mirrors."

Heard's version cost a laboratory its DNA (Heard, p. 160): "After thorough washing with Eliminase, epibionts were scraped from the kelp and processed for DNA amplification and sequencing." The washing was meant to apply to the scraper, not to the epibionts. A reader who follows the grammar destroys the sample. This is the class of error that leads readers to infer something other than what the writer intended.

## 3. Actor and action, topic and stress

**Keep actors next to actions.** Readers "are hardwired to look for actions and actors" and are left hanging when the two are separated (Heard, pp. 168-169). [Firm]

Heard's example (Heard, p. 168), with 31 words between subject and verb:

- Before: "Three results—that wounded trees were attacked by more insects, that this pattern was stronger at sparsely forested sites, and that more insects emerged from trees on the downwind side of the plots—suggest that females use plant volatiles to find hosts."
- After: "Three results suggest that females use plant volatiles to find hosts: wounded trees were attacked by more insects, this pattern was stronger at sparsely-forested sites, and more insects emerged from trees on the downwind side of the plots."

**Put familiar material in the topic position and new, important material in the stress position, then let each sentence's stress become the next sentence's topic.** Otherwise readers hold information in short-term memory and weight the wrong things (Heard, pp. 169-170, after Gopen and Swan). [Firm]

Heard's HPLC Methods paragraph (Heard, pp. 169-170):

- Before: "We used high-performance liquid chromatography to analyze reaction products. We used a Kinetex XB-C18 reverse-phase column for separation and UV absorbance to detect separated compounds. A 10% to 90% water/acetonitrile blend (20 min linear gradient) was the mobile phase. We added 10 uL of 0.1 mM phenylbutazone to each sample to serve as a run-time standard."
- After: "We analyzed reaction products using high-performance liquid chromatography. Separation was performed on a Kinetex XB-C18 reverse-phase column, and detection was by UV absorbance. The mobile phase was a water/acetonitrile blend with a 20 min linear gradient from 10% to 90% acetonitrile. To provide a run-time standard, we spiked each sample with 10 uL of 0.1 mM phenylbutazone."

Each revised sentence opens on familiar material and closes on the new item. The first sentence's stress (chromatography) becomes the second sentence's topic. Note that the revision keeps two passive constructions, because they serve the arrangement.

For noun strings, nominalizations, and abstract nouns, see words.md.

## 4. Voice

**Write in the active voice unless the passive demonstrably serves that sentence (ruling 2).** All four books agree on the default. Strunk: "The active voice is usually more direct and vigorous than the passive" (Strunk, rule 10). Heard: "use the active voice unless you are sure that the passive makes a particular sentence more effective" (Heard, p. 166). Alley: "Some scientists and engineers subscribe to the myth that all verbs should be passive voice. That is nonsense" (Alley, p. 53). Zinsser calls the difference between an active-verb style and a passive-verb style, in clarity and vigor, "the difference between life and death for a writer" (Zinsser, p. 68).

**Heard's five reasons for the active voice:** it is shorter (8 percent in his worked example), easier to read, more engaging, more vivid, because the passive "forces the use of vague and dreary verbs," and more honest (Heard, pp. 164-166). His worked case is a six-sentence Methods paragraph on marking, wounding, and felling spruce trees, printed in both voices; the active version is seven words shorter and replaces repeated *were* with *selected, marked, wounded, felled, cut, returned, held* (Heard, p. 164).

**Use the passive for Heard's three jobs:** directing attention to the thing you want in the topic position, obscuring an actor nobody cares about, and avoiding a complex or unfamiliar subject (Heard, p. 166). His third case (Heard, p. 166):

- Active but unwieldy: "Buprestid and cerambycid beetles, woodwasps, and several other taxa that damage trees or that vector pathogenic fungi attacked the wounded trees."
- Passive and clearer: "The wounded trees were attacked by buprestid and cerambycid beetles, woodwasps, and several other taxa that damage trees or that vector pathogenic fungi."

**Apply Strunk's topic test: which noun has to be the subject of this sentence?** (Strunk, rule 10.)

- "The dramatists of the Restoration are little esteemed to-day."
- "Modern readers have little esteem for the dramatists of the Restoration."

The first is right in a paragraph about the dramatists; the second is right in a paragraph about the tastes of modern readers. The need to make a particular word the subject often settles the voice (Strunk, rule 10).

**Choose the natural voice, and let inanimate objects act within what they can do.** Alley's criterion is naturalness plus keeping the focus on the study's subject: the wildebeest calf, not the painted dogs (Alley, pp. 53-54). His calibration scale for inanimate actors: "The oscilloscope displayed the voltage" is comfortable, "the oscilloscope measured" is marginal, and "the oscilloscope calculated" draws a rebuke. Standard acceptable forms are "This paper presents . . .", "Figure 1 shows . . .", and "The results indicate . . ." (Alley, p. 55). [Firm principle, per-instance judgment]

**Do not let one passive depend directly on another.** "Gold was not allowed to be exported" becomes "It was forbidden to export gold" (Strunk, rule 10).

**Do not make a noun that names the whole action the subject of a passive.** The verb is then left with nothing to do (Strunk, rule 10): "A survey of this region was made in 1900" becomes "This region was surveyed in 1900"; "Confirmation of these reports cannot be obtained" becomes "These reports cannot be confirmed."

**Replace perfunctory "there is" and "could be heard" constructions with a real verb.** "There were a great number of dead leaves lying on the ground" becomes "Dead leaves covered the ground" (Strunk, rule 10; Alley, pp. 52-53).

## 5. First person

**Use the first person judiciously, and keep the emphasis on the work (ruling 3).** "As long as the emphasis remains on your work and not you, nothing is inherently wrong with using *I* or *we*" (Alley, p. 56). Avoiding it produces "the authors assumed that" and the "It force" ("It was determined that . . ."), which lets authors shirk responsibility (Alley, pp. 55-56). [Firm on permissibility]

- Before: "In that an effort to identify a specific control circuit responsible for the failure of the gear box was unsuccessful, it was determined appropriate to resurvey the collector field for torque tube damage."
- After: "Because we could not locate the control circuit responsible for the gear box failure, we surveyed the collector field again for damage to the torque tubes." (Alley, p. 56)

**Do not open a sentence with "I" or "we" by default.** The first position carries heavy emphasis, so put the first person after an introductory word, phrase, or clause (Alley, p. 57). [Firm]

**Use an infinitive-phrase opener as the quiet way in.** "To test this process, we performed experiments in flow tubes" answers why and carries the first person without emphasizing it; Alley notes it was Pauling's habit (Alley, pp. 57, 77).

**Heard's argument for naming the actor:** the nineteenth-century convention of the actorless passive is pretense, since "trees don't fell themselves!" (Heard, p. 165).

**Zinsser's case, and his concession.** He urges writers "to use 'I' and 'me' and 'we' and 'us'" against the professorial "one" and the impersonal "it is" (Zinsser, p. 21). Where the venue forbids it, "at least think 'I' while you write, or write the first draft in the first person and then take the 'I's out" (Zinsser, p. 22). He concedes the valid objections plainly: "'I' can be a self-indulgence and a cop-out" (Zinsser, p. 22).

**The stated exception is the external company report**, where "we" is ambiguous between the authors and the company (Alley, p. 55). House style adds two more limits: never use "we" to narrate drafting, and never to promise later content (see house-style.md).

## 6. Positive form

**Make definite assertions.** Avoid tame, colorless, hesitating, noncommittal language. Use "not" as a means of denial or in antithesis, never as a means of evasion (Strunk, rule 11). [Firm]

- "He was not very often on time." becomes "He usually came late."
- "He did not think that studying Latin was much use." becomes "He thought the study of Latin useless."

**Express even a negative in positive form where a word exists:** not honest becomes dishonest; not important becomes trifling; did not remember becomes forgot; did not pay any attention to becomes ignored; did not have much confidence in becomes distrusted (Strunk, rule 11). The reader "is dissatisfied with being told only what is not; he wishes to be told what is" (Strunk, rule 11).

**Keep the negative when it carries antithesis.** "Not charity, but simple justice" is strong, and so is a negative word other than *not*: "The sun never sets upon the British flag" (Strunk, rule 11).

For hedges, qualifiers, and inflation ("clearly demonstrate", "unambiguous"), see words.md and ruling 1.

## 7. Tense

**Use the simple past for what you did, and the present for what is true now.** Heard's split: simple past in Methods and Results, present for statistical results and for references to figures and tables, because "at the moment of reading, the difference is still significant and the figure still compares impacts" (Heard, pp. 162-163). [Firm]

**Choose citation tense to signal your attitude to the cited work** (Heard, pp. 163-164). The same finding, three ways:

- Simple past, emphasizing the act of reporting (use for history, thin documentation, or doubt): "Ng (2006) reported preference by a caterpillar for phosphorus-enriched leaves."
- Present perfect, suggesting continuity and credence: "Several reports have documented herbivore preferences for phosphorus-enriched leaves (e.g., Ng 2006, Muñoz 2012)."
- Simple present, treating it as established knowledge: "Herbivores prefer phosphorus-enriched plant tissues (e.g., Ng 2006)."

**Keep one tense in a summary.** Shifting between tenses "gives the appearance of uncertainty and irresolution" (Strunk, rule 17). Strunk's related instruction is worth keeping for review writing: say once, at the outset, that what follows is summary, then stop inserting "the author also thinks" and "the speaker went on to say" (Strunk, rule 17).

**Maintain one reference frame per document:** events during the work in past tense, prior work in the pluperfect, timeless facts in the present (Alley, p. 263).

## 8. Ambiguity and pronouns

**Choose words that have a single interpretation here.** *As* can mean "while" or "because," and "having the meaning teeter in two ways is unacceptable" (Alley, p. 40).

- Before: ". . . to reduce the vibration of the fan at the exhaust as the exhaust ducting was cracking."
- After: ". . . because the exhaust ducting was cracking." (Alley, p. 40)

**Leave no momentary doubt about what a pronoun refers to.** Alley adopts Fowler's rule and its remedy: repeat the noun if necessary (Alley, p. 42).

- Before: "Because the receiver presented the radiometer with a high-flux environment, it was mounted in a silver-plated stainless steel container."
- After: "Because the receiver presented the radiometer with a high-flux environment, the radiometer was mounted in a silver-plated stainless steel container." (Alley, p. 42)

**Never use a standalone "this" as a pronoun; let "this" point.** Readers cannot tell whether it means the last noun, the subject, or the previous sentence's whole idea, and Alley reads the habit as "a sign that the author has become tired" (Alley, pp. 42-44). His instruction is to stop using *this* as a pronoun and let it "do what it does best," which is point at a noun (Alley, p. 42). [The book's strongest micro-rule]

- Before: ". . . This places an upper limit on the concentration of olefins . . ."
- After: "The chromatogram has no peaks in the olefinic region. . . . This chromatogram finding places an upper limit on the olefin concentration—no more than 0.01 percent." (Alley, pp. 42-43)

**Watch what the reader's ear is still holding.** Zinsser's pair (Zinsser, pp. 87-88): "The tragic hero of the play is Othello. Small and malevolent, Iago feeds his jealous suspicions." His gloss: "The name lingering in the reader's ear is Othello; the reader naturally assumes that Othello is small and malevolent." Read aloud, remembering where you left the reader in the previous sentence.

## 9. Parallel form

**Express coordinate ideas in similar form.** Likeness of form lets the reader recognize likeness of content and function; varying the form for variety makes the writer look undecided (Strunk, rule 15).

- Before: "Formerly, science was taught by the textbook method, while now the laboratory method is employed."
- After: "Formerly, science was taught by the textbook method; now it is taught by the laboratory method." (Strunk, rule 15)

**Use an article or preposition either once before the first term or before every term.** "The French, the Italians, Spanish, and Portuguese" becomes "The French, the Italians, the Spanish, and the Portuguese" (Strunk, rule 15).

**Follow correlative expressions with the same grammatical construction.** *Both and*, *not but*, *not only but also*, *either or*, *first second third* all take matched parts of speech (Strunk, rule 15).

- "A time not for words, but action." becomes "A time not for words, but for action." (Strunk, rule 15)
- "Our goal is either to predict or measure the speed." becomes "Our goal is either to predict or to measure the speed." (Alley, p. 239)
- "Animals in contact with the oil can develop rashes, sores, and may leave the area." becomes "Animals in contact with the oil not only can develop rashes and sores, but also may leave the area." (Alley, p. 238)

Alley rates faulty parallelism as an error that distracts, and notes it is worst in correlative pairs (Alley, pp. 238-239).

**Avoid a succession of loose sentences.** A loose sentence here means two coordinate clauses with the second introduced by a conjunction or relative. One is unexceptionable; a series "soon becomes monotonous and tedious" (Strunk, rule 14). Strunk's exhibit is a concert notice whose five consecutive sentences each join two coordinate clauses, with *and*, *while*, or *when*, producing "mechanical symmetry and sing-song." The repair is to recast enough of them, using simple sentences, two clauses joined by a semicolon, or periodic sentences, "whichever best represent the real relations of the thought" (Strunk, rule 14).

## 10. Punctuation that changes meaning

**Put a comma after every introductory phrase or clause.** Alley breaks with the general textbooks here and says so: "Although general writing textbooks often say that a comma after an introductory phrase or clause is optional unless ambiguity would occur, I assert that the complexity of engineering and science makes this comma almost mandatory" (Alley, p. 243). His supporting rule: "It is not incorrect to have a comma after an introductory phrase or clause, even when the comma is unnecessary" (Alley, p. 45). [Firm]

- "After cooling the exhaust gases continue to expand . . ." becomes "After cooling, the exhaust gases continue to expand . . ."
- "With the lid off the reactor core was exposed . . ." becomes "With the lid off, the reactor core was exposed . . ." (Alley, p. 44)

*However* in the sense "on the other hand" takes a following comma, with "no exceptions" (Alley, pp. 44-45).

**Enclose a parenthetic expression between two commas, never one.** Whether the interruption is slight or considerable, the writer "must never insert one comma and omit the other"; "Marjorie's husband, Colonel Nelson paid us a visit yesterday" is indefensible (Strunk, rule 3). Alley's version: "The new conductor, which will be available next week contains barium and copper" becomes ". . . next week, contains barium and copper" (Alley, p. 244). A single parenthetical comma should not sit between the subject and the main verb (Alley, p. 244). Strunk's rule 3 also carries the restrictive test: a clause that identifies which one you mean takes no commas ("The candidate who best meets these requirements will obtain the place"); a clause that merely adds a supplementary statement takes them ("The audience, which had at first been indifferent, became more and more interested").

**Place a comma before a conjunction that introduces a coordinate clause** (Strunk, rule 4): "The situation is perilous, but there is still one chance of escape." Strunk adds that *and* is the least specific connective, and that such sentences often improve when the first clause becomes subordinate: "Although the situation is perilous, there is still one chance of escape."

**Do not join independent clauses with a comma.** Without a conjunction, the mark is a semicolon or a period (Strunk, rule 5). Alley calls the run-on the severest error and gives four corrections for "No cure for Alzheimer's exists, however, scientists have isolated the gene that causes it" (Alley, pp. 233-234, 239-240):

1. Two sentences: "No cure for Alzheimer's exists. However, scientists have isolated the gene that causes it."
2. A semicolon: ". . . exists; however, scientists have isolated . . ."
3. A coordinating conjunction: ". . . exists, but scientists have isolated . . ."
4. A dependent clause, which Alley calls the most sophisticated: "Although no cure for Alzheimer's exists, scientists have isolated the gene that causes it."

**Do not break sentences in two.** Do not use periods for commas: "He was an interesting talker. A man who had traveled all over the world and lived in half a dozen countries" (Strunk, rule 6). A fragment used for deliberate emphasis is permissible, but only where the emphasis is warranted and no reader will suspect a blunder (Strunk, rule 6).

**Use the serial comma.** In a series of three or more terms with a single conjunction, put a comma after each term except the last (Strunk, rule 2). Alley's reason is specific to science: the list turns ambiguous without it (Alley, pp. 45-46).

- Before: ". . . hydrostatic energy in servos and logic circuits and sliding friction energy in clutches."
- After: ". . . in servos and logic circuits, and sliding friction energy in clutches." (Alley, p. 45)

**Make complex lists explicit with a colon.** "In our study, we examined neat methanol and ethanol and methanol and ethanol with 10 percent water" has four readings; "we examined four fuels: neat methanol, neat ethanol, methanol with 10 percent water, and ethanol with 10 percent water" has one (Alley, p. 46).

**Use a colon only after a complete sentence.** "The five types of Marsupialia studied were: opossums, bandicoots, koalas, wombats, and kangaroos" needs the colon deleted, because what stands to its left is not a sentence (Alley, p. 245).

**Use the semicolon sparingly (ruling 7).** It is allowed for two closely linked independent clauses and for list items that themselves contain commas (Alley, p. 246; Strunk, rule 5). It is not a habit: Alley calls it "fussy" and notes many professionals would use a period, and Zinsser says it slows prose "to a Victorian pace" while admitting he uses it "fairly often" himself (Alley, p. 246; Zinsser, p. 73).

**Use "that" for a defining clause and "which" for a clause you set off with commas.** Zinsser's two-minute version: "use 'that' unless it makes your meaning ambiguous"; if the sentence needs a comma it probably needs "which," because which "identifies, locates, explains or qualifies the phrase before the comma" (Zinsser, pp. 75-76). Alley states the same test and allows one stylistic exception, when two *that* clauses collide (Alley, p. 262).

**Use no em dashes in manuscript prose.** House style overrides both Zinsser, who recommends the dash in two patterns, and Alley, who treats it as flexible punctuation. Recast with commas, parentheses, a colon, a semicolon, or a full stop. See house-style.md.

## 11. Grammar as convention

**Treat grammar as convention, not as morality.** "Think of grammar instead as a set of agreed-upon conventions by which your reader can easily assign to a sequence of words the meaning that you intended" (Heard, p. 160).

**Break a rule only when you can name the rule and say why breaking it helps.** Otherwise the violation reads as sloppiness, because readers expect the conventions (Heard, pp. 161-162). [Firm] His licensed example: "to boldly go where no man has gone before" emphasizes the boldness rather than the going, and the corrected "to go boldly" falls flat (Heard, p. 161).

**Sort violations into three classes before you spend effort on them** (Heard, pp. 160-162):

1. Errors that change meaning. The Eliminase sentence in section 2 is the model. Fix these first.
2. Errors that merely sound unprofessional. "Image post-processing was required to resolve the planet from it's rings"; "One rat died prior to taking final blood-pressure measurements." Readers will not believe rats take blood pressure, "but they will cheerfully believe that the writer can't construct a coherent sentence" (Heard, p. 161).
3. Violations that are cosmetic. Split infinitives and terminal prepositions live here.

Alley's parallel scheme is useful for triage because he assigns severity to individual items: errors that **unsettle** readers and cost you their confidence, errors that **distract** and make readers reread, and issues that are **cosmetic**, which he calls the "tuxedo rules" (Alley, pp. 236-237, 249). His own posture is dual: as an author he avoids tuxedo violations to spare "the temperamental few"; as an editor he lets them pass and saves comments for larger fish (Alley, pp. 236, 239).

**Begin a sentence with "Because" or "However" when the sentence needs it (ruling 4).** "Do not be swayed by the myth that you cannot begin a sentence with *because*"; no respected grammar book gives that advice, and *Nature* runs many such sentences (Alley, pp. 78, 139). Sentence-initial *however* is accepted by almost all editors (Alley, p. 238). Strunk's rule that *however* meaning "nevertheless" must not come first is historical, not applied (Strunk, "However").

**Treat split infinitives and terminal prepositions as cosmetic.** Alley: not an error today unless more than one word intervenes (Alley, p. 240); a terminal preposition is cosmetic (Alley, p. 239). Heard: the split-infinitive rule "doesn't actually make much sense" (Heard, p. 161). Strunk's entry, which says the construction "is in disfavor and is avoided by nearly all careful writers," is historical, not applied (Strunk, "Split Infinitive").

**Starting with "But" is available, and the venue decides.** Zinsser: "There's no stronger word at the start," and do not open with "however," which "hangs there like a wet dishrag" (Zinsser, p. 74). Alley disagrees for journals, siding with them against opening conjunctions and preferring "Also," "In addition," "However," and "Conversely" (Alley, pp. 237-238; Table 1-1, p. 12). Precedence puts Alley first for a journal paper; Zinsser's permission stands for chapters and essays where the venue allows it.

**Other Strunk items marked historical, not applied:** generic "he" for everyone, and the shall-versus-will distinction (Strunk, "Shall, Will"; see Heard, p. 24 n.2, who adopts singular *they*).

| affect / effect | *affect* means to influence; *effect* as a noun means result, as a verb means to bring about | U | Alley, pp. 250-251; Strunk, "Effect" |
| principal / principle | *principal* is chief or a sum of money; *principle* is a rule | D | Alley, pp. 250, 260 |
| due to / because of | *due to* modifies a noun after a form of *to be*; *because of* modifies a verb and can open a sentence | N in Alley, stricter in Strunk | Alley, pp. 250, 252-253; Strunk, "Due to" |
| its / it's | *it's* is only "it is"; "Accept them, learn them, and never confuse them" | sounds unprofessional (Heard) | Alley, p. 257; Heard, p. 161 |

| fewer / less, number / amount | count nouns take *fewer* and *number*; quantities take *less* and *amount*; money and time count as continuous | | Alley, pp. 252, 255; Strunk, "Less" |

| continual / continuous | *continuous* means uninterrupted; "continuous improvements" exaggerates unless nobody sleeps | | Alley, pp. 16, 253 |
| different from | write *different from*, *other than*, or *unlike* | | Alley, p. 254; Strunk, "Different than" |
| farther / further | *farther* for physical distance, *further* for degree or extent | | Alley, p. 255 |
| than / then | comparison versus time; a spell-checker does not catch it | | Alley, pp. 225, 262 |
| criterion / criteria, medium / media, phenomenon / phenomena, stratum / strata | keep the singular and plural distinct | | Alley, pp. 254, 258, 260 |
| data | plural in formal prose, or sidestep with "data point" or "the dataset" (ruling 5) | | Alley, p. 254; Strunk, "Data" |

## 13. Checklist

Run these on any draft passage at the sentence layer.

1. Does each sentence carry one idea, and does the document's average length sit in the teens? (Alley, pp. 20, 26; Zinsser, pp. 266-267)
2. Does any sentence over about 25 words announce its length at the start, with a dependent clause or similar opener? (Alley, p. 26)
3. Is the main verb early rather than parked before the period, and does the new information sit at the end? (Alley, pp. 50-51; Strunk, rule 18)
4. Does each subject sit next to its verb, with no stack of clauses or prepositional phrases wedged between them? (Heard, pp. 168-169; Strunk, rule 16)
5. Does each sentence open on familiar material and close on the new item, so its stress becomes the next sentence's topic? (Heard, pp. 169-170)
6. Is every passive doing one of Heard's three jobs or passing Strunk's topic test, with no passive depending on another? (Heard, p. 166; Strunk, rule 10; ruling 2)
7. Is the first person measured, never sentence-initial by default, never narrating the drafting? (Alley, pp. 56-57; ruling 3; house-style.md)
8. Does every pronoun, "this" and "it" first, point to a noun the reader cannot mistake? (Alley, pp. 42-44; Zinsser, pp. 87-88)
9. Are the tenses consistent: past for what you did, present for what is true and for figures, tables, and statistical results? (Heard, pp. 162-163; Alley, p. 263; Strunk, rule 17)
10. Are coordinate ideas and correlative pairs in matching grammatical form? (Strunk, rule 15; Alley, pp. 238-239)
11. Does every introductory phrase take a comma, every parenthetical two commas, and every pair of independent clauses a conjunction, semicolon, or period rather than a bare comma? (Alley, pp. 45, 239-240; Strunk, rules 3 to 6)
12. Is the passage free of em dashes, and does every usage-watchlist item in it survive a check? (house-style.md; Alley, pp. 249-265)
