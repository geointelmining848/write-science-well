# Words

Purpose: choose, cut, and check words so that each one in a scientific document does work.

Lead source: Zinsser (chs. 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 10, 16) and Alley (Lessons 2, 4, 6, and the pretentious words and arrogant
phrases of Lesson 9). Baseline: Strunk (rules 12 and 13, chapter V). Supplement: Heard (chs. 19 and 20, and the numbers
rules of ch. 12). All four books converge on this layer, so most rules below carry more than one citation.

## 1. Strip the sentence to its cleanest components

Cut every element that does no work. "Clutter is the disease of American writing. We are a society strangling in
unnecessary words, circular constructions, pompous frills and meaningless jargon" (Zinsser, p. 7). Hunt the four
adulterants by name: "Every word that serves no function, every long word that could be a short word, every adverb that
carries the same meaning that's already in the verb, every passive construction that leaves the reader unsure of who is
doing what" (Zinsser, p. 7). Adverbs and voice belong to sentences.md.

All four books state the rule. Strunk: "Omit needless words," which requires "that he make every word tell" (rule 13).
Alley: needless complexity is "the most egregious language problem in scientific writing" (p. 20). Heard sets the unit
below the word: "every character of your text should be forced to justify its existence" (p. 183); of about a thousand
manuscripts he has reviewed, "all but a handful should have been shorter" (p. 182). Ask Zinsser's four questions of any
paragraph you suspect: "Is every word doing new work? Can any thought be expressed with more economy? Is anything pompous
or pretentious or faddish? Are you hanging on to something useless just because you think it's beautiful?" (p. 17). Cut
content while you plan and draft, and cut expression only in revision, because hunting economy in a first draft costs
momentum (Heard, p. 183; see revision.md).

## 2. Concrete before abstract

Prefer the specific to the general, the definite to the vague, the concrete to the abstract. Readers think in particulars,
so an abstract word must be turned into an image before it can be used (Strunk, rule 12).

| Instead of (Strunk, rule 12) | Write |
|---|---|
| A period of unfavorable weather set in. | It rained every day for a week. |
| He showed satisfaction as he took possession of his well-earned reward. | He grinned as he pocketed the coin. |

Use concrete nouns, and anchor an abstract noun with a definition or an example. Alley lists the abstract nouns that creep
into science: ability, approach, capability, concept, environment, factor, nature, parameter (p. 59). On a manuscript
that wrote "environment" for the place where crops grow, his question is the test to reuse: "What is wrong with the word
farm?" (p. 59). [firm] Before (Alley, p. 58): "The existing nature of Mount St. Helens' volcanic ash spewage was
handled through the applied use of computer modeling capabilities." After: "With supercomputers, we modeled how much ash
spewed from Mount St. Helens."

Replace concept nouns with people doing things; a concept noun leaves a sentence with nobody in it and no working verb
(Zinsser, p. 76). Before: "The common reaction is incredulous laughter." After: "Most people just laugh with
disbelief." His warning for an abstract-heavy draft: "Don't get caught holding a bag full of abstract nouns" (p. 77).
Break up noun chains too, which he calls creeping nounism: "Communication facilitation skills development intervention,"
glossed "Not a person in sight, or a working verb. I think it's a program to help students write better" (pp. 77-78).
Stacked modifiers are the same fault (Alley, pp. 24-25; see sentences.md).

Turn nominalizations back into verbs. They "replace lively verbs with passive, bloated nouns, camouflaging the characters
and actions," and readers understand best "when nouns name the characters and verbs express the actions" (Heard,
pp. 178-179). [firm, with named exceptions] Alley makes the same repair on verbs buried in nouns (pp. 51-52).

| Instead of (Heard, p. 179; Alley, pp. 51-52) | Write |
|---|---|
| We conducted an analysis of the data | We analyzed the data |
| The adaptive lens had superior performance | The adaptive lens performed better |
| Our intention is to | We intend to |
| Liu (1995) provided a review of | Liu (1995) reviewed |
| made the measurement of | measured |
| is dependent on | depends on |

## 3. Needless words and phrases

Delete the phrase that has pushed out the short word meaning the same thing. "Writing improves in direct ratio to the
number of things we can keep out of it that shouldn't be there" (Zinsser, p. 13).

| Instead of (Strunk, rule 13) | Write |
|---|---|
| the question as to whether | whether |
| there is no doubt but that | no doubt |
| used for fuel purposes | used for fuel |
| he is a man who | he |
| in a hasty manner | hastily |
| this is a subject which | this subject |
| the fact that he had not succeeded | his failure |
| His brother, who is a member of the same firm | His brother, a member of the same firm |

Strunk singles out one construction: "In especial the expression *the fact that* should be revised out of every sentence
in which it occurs" (rule 13). "Who is" and "which was" are usually deletable too. Prefer the short word to the long word
that means the same thing, keeping the long one only when it brings necessary precision (Zinsser, p. 16; Alley,
pp. 21-22; Heard, p. 177). Both tables below draw on those pages and on Alley, p. 62, and Heard, p. 184.

| Instead of | Write | Instead of | Write |
|---|---|---|---|
| assistance | help | implement | do |
| numerous | many | sufficient | enough |
| facilitate | ease | attempt | try |
| remainder | rest | referred to as | called |
| initial | first | utilize | use |

| Instead of | Write | Instead of | Write |
|---|---|---|---|
| with the possible exception of | except | at this point in time | now |
| due to the fact that | because | in the event that | if |
| owing to the fact that | because | in the vicinity of | near |
| until such time as | until | has the ability to, is able to | can |
| for the purpose of | for | the majority of | most |

Delete the cluster that announces what you are about to do: "I might add," "It should be pointed out," "It is interesting
to note" (Zinsser, p. 16). Three books rule the same way: "If it is interesting to note, make it interesting" (Zinsser,
p. 16); "Instead of announcing that what you are about to tell is interesting, make it so" (Strunk, ch. V,
"Interesting"); and Alley's writing zeroes, among them as a matter of fact, it is noteworthy that, the course of, the fact
that, the presence of (p. 61). Cut the preposition welded to the verb: head up, free up, order up, face up to (Zinsser,
p. 13). Delete redundancies, where a modifier repeats the noun or verb: already existing, basic fundamentals,
completely eliminate, currently underway, empty space, mix together, period of time, still persists (Alley, p. 60).
Heard's tautologous modifiers are the same list from another angle (completely finish, may potentially, ultimate result,
blue in color), beside his empty modifiers (really, basically, quite, various) and his padding (needless to say, for all
intents and purposes) (p. 184).

Attack Heard's eleven text-bulgers when you revise for length: passive voice, nominalizations, long words, roundabout
phrases, tautologous modifiers, empty modifiers, padding, hedging, metadiscourse, parentheticals, redundancy
(pp. 183-186). Check metadiscourse skeptically ("We believe that our results establish that salinity is..." says no
more than "Salinity is...") and ask of every parenthetical whether it is worth the demand it makes (pp. 185-186).
[judgment]

Learn your own bulging habits. Heard's worked case is Chandra Moffat's conference abstract, cut from 394 to 276 words, 30
percent shorter, with no content lost (pp. 186-189). Roundabout openings compressed, three aims collapsed to two, a
four-sentence result collapsed to one clause, and self-protective framing went ("Here, we present the first study, to our
knowledge, showing..." became "We demonstrate, for the first time..."). Her habits were redundancy and roundabout phrases,
not hedging (p. 189). Cut bureaucratic waste at the section level as well: asking who the audience is often deletes
whole sections, as it did for Alley's preface "written to itself" (pp. 65-67).

## 4. Qualifiers, hedges, and intensifiers

Apply ruling 1: hedge exactly to the evidence (Heard), cut habit hedges (Zinsser), attack inflation as hard as caution
(Alley). The two failures are opposite and both cost credibility. Prune the little qualifiers: a bit, a little, sort of,
kind of, rather, quite, very, too, pretty much, in a sense (Zinsser, p. 71). The damage is cumulative: "Every little
qualifier whittles away some fraction of the reader's trust" (pp. 71-72). Count them before you defend them: his
exhibit is Elliot Richardson's "on balance, affirmative action has, I think, been a qualified success," a 13-word sentence
with five hedging words (pp. 23-24).

Keep the hedges that carry information about claim strength. Heard alone defends hedging: hedges are "essential... to
express the precise strength (or limitations) of the claims you're making" (p. 184). His target is excess, and excess
breeds: "hedges are like potato chips: once you write one, it's hard to stop, and they expand your text like your
waistline" (p. 184). Emphatics fall under the same rule (clearly, primary, major), because excess hedging "makes it
difficult for readers to take your argument seriously" and excess emphasis does the same from the other side
(pp. 184-185). Before (p. 184): "(Observation) could reasonably be assumed to possibly occur by chance." After: "The
evidence that (observation) was nonrandom was modest (P = 0.06)." Four hedges become one number and one word that reports
the strength of the evidence.

Cut "very," most adjectives, and nearly all adverbs. "In a sense, the word *very* cripples the word that it modifies"
(Alley, p. 63): calling an early result "very important" spends the word "important" for the rest of the document.
Alley quotes Twain: "When you catch an adjective, kill it" (p. 63). Strunk agrees without the vehemence: use "very"
sparingly and "where emphasis is necessary, use words strong in themselves" (ch. V, "Very"), avoid "so" as an intensifier
(ch. V, "So"), and do not let "certainly" intensify every statement (ch. V, "Certainly").

Never exaggerate. Scientific readers count things, so "countless" and "thorough" raise the question of what else you are
stretching. Reserve "prove" for a claim at the standard of a geometric proof and "optimal" for a demonstrated maximum
(Alley, pp. 19-20). Delete "thorough" from "our thorough research of the literature," because "whether the research is
'thorough' is the reader's call" (p. 19). When you edit an author's own finished prose, the plain emphasis words the
author uses deliberately stay (house-style.md, "The author's voice"); that file also rules that a hedge belongs only on
what the analysis genuinely declines to resolve.

## 5. Arrogant and pretentious phrases

Cut the phrases that claim agreement instead of earning it: "as is well known," "of course," "clearly demonstrate,"
"unambiguous," "it is obvious" (Alley, pp. 163-164). They assume a superior position, defy the reader to question the
work, and invite the rebuttal they were meant to prevent. "Scientific work should stand on its own merit without cajoling
from the writer" (p. 164). [firm] Before (p. 164): "The results clearly demonstrate the ability of Raman
spectroscopy to provide unambiguous chemical compound identifications from oxides as they grow on a metal surface." After:
"The results show that Raman spectroscopy can identify chemical compounds from oxides that are growing on a metal
surface."

Cut pretentious words, which "smack of a pseudo-intellectuality that poses a barrier between writer and reader" (Alley,
p. 162). His list with its reasons: approximately (write "about" for a rough estimate), component (part), facilitate
(help, cause, bring about), any -ability noun such as manufacturability (rewrite with "can" plus a stronger verb), any
-ize verb such as prioritize (rank), any -ization noun such as utilization (use) (pp. 162-163). Zinsser calls the same
words fad words: paradigm, parameter, prioritize, potentialize, and the verbs dialogue and interface (p. 16); his
journalese is the wider habit, "a quilt of instant words patched together out of other parts of speech" (p. 33). Before
(Alley, p. 163): "The elevated temperatures of the liquid propellant fuel facilitated an unscheduled irrevocable
disassembly." After: "The higher temperatures caused the liquid propellant to explode."

Retire Strunk's hackneyed words, which survive in scientific prose a century later.

| Instead of (Strunk, ch. V) | Write |
|---|---|
| His superior training was the great factor in his winning the match. | He won the match by being better trained. |
| Acts of a hostile character, acts of a hostile nature | Hostile acts |
| In many cases, the rooms were poorly ventilated. | Many of the rooms were poorly ventilated. |
| Dayton has adopted the commission system of government. | Dayton has adopted government by commission. |
| He is studying along the line of French literature. | He is studying French literature. |

Also on his list: "feature," which "usually adds nothing to the sentence in which it occurs"; "one of the most" as an
opening formula, "threadbare and forcible-feeble"; and "worth while" as vague approval (ch. V). Strunk's dated usage rules
elsewhere in chapter V (shall versus will, the generic "he," the ban on the split infinitive) are historical, not applied.
One sophisticated word per section is still fine: "in being sophisticated, you need not be complicated" (Alley, p. 22).
[judgment]

## 6. Precision of word choice

Do not use a word unless you are sure of its meaning; look it up when in doubt. A reader who knows the real meaning loses
confidence in everything nearby: "plethora" means too many, not many, so "a plethora of designs" becomes "a host of
designs" (Alley, p. 16). Use a dictionary, not a thesaurus, because "while a thesaurus identifies possible right words,
a good dictionary reveals the subtle differences between those possibilities" (Alley, p. 18). Zinsser allows Roget's as
a memory aid, then sends you to the dictionary for the distinction (pp. 35-36), and Heard says to use a dictionary
"without embarrassment" (p. 175).

Do not hunt synonyms for variety; repeat the right word. Substituting "free convection" for "natural convection" confuses
readers new to the vocabulary, and "synonym writing is not strong writing" (Alley, pp. 17-18). Zinsser's specimen is a
tennis player who becomes, inside one paragraph, "the gangling junior, the Memphis native, the Exeter graduate, the
racquet ace and the redhead"; repeat the name instead, because "the cure is worse than the ailment" (pp. 181-182). He
also refused an editor's "correction" that replaced a deliberate repetition with a synonym (p. 291). Heard gives the
science version: write "polyacrylamide gel" once in the Methods and "gel" thereafter (pp. 177-178). House style agrees
and goes further: one object, one name, document-wide (house-style.md).

Check connotation, not only denotation: "cheap" denotes inexpensive and connotes shoddy, and "obvious" either insults the
reader or raises the question of why the detail is there (Alley, pp. 18-19). Remember also that meaning is a convention
among readers, not a property you assign. Heard's soybean ladder runs complex carbohydrate, polysaccharide, starch,
amylose: every rung is correct and each says something different (pp. 174-176). Choose the word that fits your story,
but never one that misleads: "if your assay measured only amylose, then amylose must be your choice" (p. 176). The same
holds for "significant," which means one thing to a statistician and another to a lay reader, a gap that has repeatedly
misled medical reporting.

Use precise verbs. "Don't say that the president of the company stepped down. Did he resign? Did he retire? Did he get
fired?" (Zinsser, p. 69). Prefer short Anglo-Saxon words: of the 701 words in Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address, 505
have one syllable and 122 have two (p. 69).

## 7. Jargon and technical terms

Apply ruling 13: use technical terms when the audience holds them, define each once, and never use one as a crutch. The
responsibility sits with the writer: "In scientific writing, the author (not the reader) bears the responsibility of
bridging the language gap to the audience" (Alley, p. 99).

Use jargon only where it makes reading more efficient for these readers. It is efficient inside a facility and alienating
outside it, and a report written in local abbreviations "reads like a cryptogram" to anyone else. "Do not assume that
jargon is inherently bad" (Alley, pp. 99-100). [firm, with his own non-absolutist qualifier] Heard draws the line by
cost and benefit: technical words used for an audience that knows them "both save length and enhance clarity," and become
jargon only "if they are used when a simpler word suffices, or when forced on readers unfamiliar with them" (p. 177).

Apply Zinsser's two-part test to a suspect term: does the word fill a real need, and does it convey a picture (pp. 41,
45)? "Printout" passes, because a computer emits a real object; "input" escaped its domain and colonized everything;
"prioritize" is jargon because it only "sounds more important than 'rank'" (p. 45). Do not treat plain style as a
concession to laymen, either; peers in neighboring specialties need it. Zinsser's exhibit is Donald Christiansen, editor
of *I.E.E.E. Spectrum*: "There are 37 different identifiable disciplines within I.E.E.E. If you can't describe something
in words, our own people can't understand each other" (p. 161). His companion rule is never to use your field's
vocabulary as a crutch (pp. 172-173). Write a formal definition as class plus differentia: name the class the term
belongs to, then add enough to separate it from everything else in that class (Alley, p. 101). Coin only indispensable
new words, since a coinage is both highly technical and unfamiliar, a double burden (Heard, p. 178).

## 8. Abbreviations

Define an abbreviation in parentheses after the first full expression, but only if the term recurs. If it appears once or
twice, spell it out. If it appears once early and often much later, define it where it is used, not where it first appears
(Alley, pp. 101-102). Treat a well-known abbreviation as the familiar term: DNA, TNT, and AIDS belong in titles,
summaries, and text as they stand, defined once for completeness (p. 102). Do not invent your own, because "newly
coined abbreviations are doubly difficult for readers and are rarely a good idea" (Heard, p. 178).

Write out Latin abbreviations, which all have plain English equivalents: e.g., et al., etc., i.e. (Alley, p. 23).
Before: "a gamma-ray line, i.e., radiation at a single gamma-ray energy level." After: "a gamma-ray line (radiation at a
single gamma-ray energy level)" (p. 242). Do not set project or software names in all capitals either, because readers
recognize words by shape, from ascenders and descenders, which capitals remove (pp. 23-24). Avoid the slash, "an ugly
piece of punctuation that adds unwanted complexity": write "without," "she or he," and either "and" or "or," or "A or B or
both" (p. 24).

## 9. Numbers in text

Put two or three numbers in a sentence and use a graphic for more, since a graphic costs the reader a navigation detour
(Heard, p. 103). A two-bar figure comparing seed mass becomes "Fertilized seeds were larger than unfertilized ones (1.8
± 0.3 g vs 0.9 ± 0.2 g)"; six means with standard deviations run together in one sentence go the other way, into a figure
(pp. 103-104). See figures-tables-equations.md for the full choice. Report only significant and necessary precision:
two significant digits for P, two or three for test statistics. Before (Heard, p. 108): "(F = 0.92238674, P = 0.7826)."
After: "F = 0.9, P = 0.8," which "carries just as much information and does so more clearly." Order numbers so the ones
you want compared come first and the supporting ones sit in parentheses: "54% vs. 34% (25/46 vs. 23/67)" emphasizes the
rates, "25/46 vs. 23/67 (54% vs. 34%)" the counts and sample sizes (p. 106). Omit redundant numbers, such as counts and
percentages on equal denominators, or any two of df, SS, and MS.

Never begin a sentence with a numeral; restructure instead. Before: "64.1 milligrams of copper corroded during the tests."
After: "During the tests, 64.1 milligrams of copper corroded" (Alley, p. 280). Write out numbers expressible in one or
two words, with these exceptions, which take numerals: illustration numbers, negative numbers, decimals, specific
measurements, percentages, consistency within a paragraph, monetary figures, and large numbers (pp. 279-280). Alley
marks the one-or-two-words rule as his own preference over the commoner "spell out below ten." Where house-style.md
sets a number rule, it governs; its starter rule is that numbers taking part in an arithmetic the prose states appear as
numerals, and purely descriptive small counts stay words.

## 10. Informality

Avoid informal expressions in articles, reports, and theses (Alley, p. 12, Table 1-1). [firm for most items; "you,"
"vs.", and "&" depend on the occasion]

| Instead of | Write | Instead of | Write |
|---|---|---|---|
| a lot | much, many | But... (sentence-initial) | However,... |
| get | obtain | vs. | versus |
| don't, isn't, can't | do not, is not, cannot | & | and |
| And... (sentence-initial) | Also,... | you | one |

Alley's footnote on "you": accepted in correspondence and instructional texts, not in articles or reports. Two
disagreements settle here. Zinsser allows contractions in nonfiction, except "I'd," "he'd," and "we'd," which are
ambiguous between "had" and "would" (p. 75); Alley keeps them out of formal scientific text, likening one to "showing
up to an interview in a t-shirt" (p. 254). For scientific documents, Alley governs. Zinsser also prefers "But" at the
start of a sentence and dislikes "however" there (p. 74), the opposite of Alley's table. Ruling 4 stands:
sentence-initial "However" and "Because" are both allowed. Sentence-initial "And" and "But" stay out of journal
papers, where Alley governs; Zinsser's "But" is available in chapters and essays where the venue allows it (see
sentences.md).

## 11. Overstatement and over-explaining

Do not put a value on a fact before the reader meets it. Cut "surprisingly," "predictably," and "of course," which "put a
value on a fact before the reader encounters the fact" (Zinsser, p. 92). The reason is the reader's share of the work:
"The reader plays a major role in the act of writing and must be given room to play it" (p. 92). Trust your material
instead: "The assumption is that fact and color are two separate ingredients. They're not; color is organic to the fact"
(p. 89). Protect credibility, which is spent all at once. "If the reader catches you in just one bogus statement that
you are trying to pass off as true, everything you write thereafter will be suspect" (Zinsser, p. 78). Alley reaches
the same place from the scientific reader's side: exaggeration raises the question of what else you are stretching, and
arrogant phrases invite rebuttal rather than warding it off (pp. 19-20, 163-164).

## Checklist

Run these on a draft once the structure is settled. Any "no" marks a place to revise.

1. Does every word do new work, with no thought that could be expressed with more economy (Zinsser, p. 17)?
2. Have you deleted the phrases that announce what you are about to say ("it is interesting to note")?
3. Does each abstract noun carry a definition or an example, or can a concrete noun replace it (Alley, p. 59)?
4. Have you converted the nominalizations, so that nouns name the characters and verbs express the actions (Heard,
   pp. 178-179)?
5. Does every hedge report the actual strength of the evidence, with none kept from habit (Heard, p. 184; Zinsser,
   pp. 71-72)?
6. Have you cut the intensifiers, "very" first, and every adjective or adverb that repeats what the noun or verb already
   says (Alley, p. 63; Strunk, ch. V, "Very")?
7. Have you removed the phrases that claim agreement ("clearly demonstrate," "as is well known") and the overstatements
   ("prove," "optimal," "countless," "thorough") (Alley, pp. 19-20, 163-164)?
8. Is every word used in the sense your readers hold, with one name held for each object (Alley, pp. 16-18; Heard,
   pp. 174-176)?
9. Does each technical term earn its place with this audience, defined once by class plus differentia (Alley,
   pp. 99-101; Heard, p. 177)?
10. Is every abbreviation defined at first use and used again later, with one-use terms spelled out and none newly coined
    (Alley, pp. 101-102; Heard, p. 178)?
11. Do the numbers run two or three per sentence, at the precision the result supports, with compared values first and no
    sentence opening on a numeral (Heard, pp. 103-108; Alley, p. 280)?
12. Have you left the reader room to judge, with no "surprisingly," "predictably," or "of course" placed in front of a
    fact (Zinsser, p. 92)?
