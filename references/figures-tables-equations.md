# Figures, tables, and equations

Purpose: decide whether a set of numbers belongs in a sentence, a table, or a figure, then design the graphic, write its caption, point at it from the text, and set equations inside sentences.

Lead source: Heard (ch. 12, on numbers, tables, figures, legends, and pointing at graphics) and Alley (Lesson 2 on illustrations and captions, Lesson 5 on equations and on connecting illustrations with text, Lesson 6 on scale, Lesson 8 on results in illustrations, Appendix D on placement and display). Supplement: house-style.md for the author's own float and equation rules.

## Choosing text, table, or figure

Settle the medium before drafting the sentence. Heard treats this as one decision with three outcomes, and the wrong outcome costs the reader either a detour or an unread pattern.

**Put two or three numbers in a sentence; use a graphic only for more.** A graphic costs the reader a navigation detour away from the sentence, and two numbers do not repay it. (Heard, p. 103) [firm]

**Use a table for exact values and for many entries; use a figure for trends and relationships, especially nonlinear ones.** Tables show pattern poorly. Figures show precision poorly, and they handle many variables poorly. (Heard, pp. 104-105) [firm]

**Present any set of numbers exactly once.** Never in the text and the table and the figure. Repetition across the three forces the reader to check whether the versions agree. (Heard, p. 105) [firm]

**Winnow the data before you choose a form.** Report the most relevant metric, or reduce the data, and park the alternatives in a supplement or in one reassuring clause. (Heard, p. 106) [firm]

**Drop redundant numbers before you build the table.** Counts and percentages on the same denominator duplicate each other, and any two of df, SS, and MS duplicate the third. (Heard, p. 106) [firm]

**Match the illustration's precision to the text's.** A schematic far more detailed than the paragraph it serves belongs in an appendix. (Alley, p. 30) [firm]

Before moving a graphic to a supplement, run Heard's three-way placement test (body, supplement, or unpublished). That test lives in structure.md; do not restate it here.

Heard's Box 12.1 gives the trade in both directions (pp. 103-104).

- Wasteful graphic to text. Before: a two-bar figure comparing seed mass in fertilized and unfertilized plants. After: "Fertilized seeds were larger than unfertilized ones (1.8 ± 0.3 g vs 0.9 ± 0.2 g)." (Heard, p. 103)
- Run-on sentence to graphic. Before: one sentence listing six grain-size means with standard deviations at six distances, on which Heard's whole comment is "Ugh!" After: "Grain sizes of lakebottom sediments decreased away from the inflow (Figure B)." (Heard, p. 104)

For how many digits to report and how to order numbers inside a sentence, see words.md.

## Designing tables

**Arrange columns left to right from familiar or independent variables to new or dependent ones.** Readers scan a table the way they read a line of text. (Heard, pp. 108-109) [firm]

**Display the comparison you want made down a column, not across a row.** Vertical comparison is easier, which is why tall narrow tables beat short wide ones. (Heard, pp. 108-109) [firm]

**Do not shrink fonts or squeeze white space to make a table fit.** Heard's reason is blunt: "compressing it will only make it unreadable" (p. 109). Cut rows or split the table instead. [firm]

**Head tables above, and keep headings short.** Position and name are the only things separating a table title from a figure legend. (Heard, p. 117; Alley, pp. 277, 283) [firm]

**Prefer a table to a vertical list.** A list gives one perspective; a table gives two or more, and it can hold columns of short parallel descriptions as well as numbers. (Alley, p. 146) [firm]

**Hold vertical lists to at most one per two or three pages.** Lists interrupt reading the way traffic lights interrupt driving, and a page of them makes a paper look like an outline. (Alley, p. 146) [firm] For lists inside running prose, see paragraphs.md.

**In a long document, put the literature review in a table, one row per paper or group, your own work on the last row.** It answers the reviewer's question about what is new at a glance. (Alley, p. 119) [judgment, "I recommend"]

Heard prints his own worst table as a confession (Box 12.2, p. 105). It was a five-by-five clutch-size matrix from Heard and Remer 1997, carrying too many digits, with bold and underlining that "didn't help nearly as much as I thought it would." His verdict on it: a figure would have shown the trends much more clearly.

## Designing figures

**Choose straightforward, familiar figure types.** Heard: "glitzy and novel approaches to figure design are more likely to impede communication than facilitate it" (pp. 109-110). [firm]

**Know which of the three classes you are drawing.** A data reproduction shows the raw record, a schematic abstracts it, and a data compilation summarizes many observations. A schematic is an interpretation, so say what it leaves out. (Heard, p. 110) [firm]

**Pick the compilation that shows the specific pattern.** Heard's Box 12.3 (p. 111) matches three: a scatterplot with a fit line for an asymptotic windspeed profile, a boxplot for volatile content of lava from three vents, and divided bars for the fates of spruce budworm larvae. Each type was chosen because it makes one pattern visible.

**Use photographs sparingly.** A photograph is usually a single datum carrying extraneous detail (Heard, pp. 110-111). Alley adds that a photograph often raises more questions than it answers: the messy bench, the lab coat, and the clipboard all compete for attention (p. 31). [firm in both]

**Disclose any enhancement of a photograph.** Undisclosed, Heard says, "the same manipulation would be misleading at best and fraudulent at worst" (pp. 110-111). [firm]

**Keep to about four panels per figure and four data traces per panel.** (Heard, p. 112) [judgment, stated as a rule of thumb]. His counter-example is a real twenty-panel figure of fish diversity against lake area with per-lake symbols in four separate keys; no reader can tell which comparison matters (Box 12.4, p. 113).

**Design for reduction: thick lines, large fonts, large symbols.** Simplify elements rather than shrink them. A reader who has to zoom has stopped reading. (Heard, p. 112) [firm]

**Hold codings constant across panels and across figures.** "Every shift in design between figures . . . asks the reader to master a new visual dialect." (Heard, p. 112) [firm]

**Put symbol keys inside the figure, not in the legend.** (Heard, pp. 112-114) [firm]

**Minimize color.** About 3% of readers have a color-vision deficiency, colors shift with light and device, and readers still print in black and white, so use color "only when there is no other way to communicate the necessary information" (Heard, p. 114). This is stricter than current practice. [firm]

**Start vertical axes at zero, give compared panels identical scales, and signal any deviation from either.** Heard draws one sun-and-shade by fertilizer dataset three ways in Box 12.5 (pp. 115-116): axes from zero, which is honest; both panels zoomed to 7 to 9.5 g, which exaggerates every difference; and panels zoomed differently, which hides the sun and shade effect while magnifying fertilization. His summary: "each pair of plots shows exactly the same data, but they give very different impressions." [firm]

**Do not draw a fit line for a non-significant relationship, and do not extend one past the data.** A line asserts a certainty the data may not carry. (Heard, pp. 114-116) [firm]

**Think before connecting sparse points.** Joining widely spaced points claims you know the shape between them. (Heard, pp. 114-116) [firm]

**Show scale with a scale bar or a familiar object.** A caption that says 39 square meters does not land; two people standing in the foreground do. (Alley, p. 105) [firm]

**Put the results you want remembered into illustrations, and keep the illustrations few.** Readers look at every illustration even when they skip sentences, but many illustrations dilute the emphasis any one of them receives. (Alley, pp. 143-144) [firm]

**Do not assume an illustration is worth a thousand words.** Alley's test is whether the image answers more questions than it raises. (Alley, p. 31) [firm]

**Challenge the software defaults.** Excel's graph defaults were built for business bar charts. Delete the chart title, since the caption does that job. Use both sets of grid lines or neither, and often neither. Delete the box border, which "adds noise." Delete the legend and label the curves directly, or park the key in dead space inside the plot. (Alley, pp. 273-274) [firm]

**Leave a white-space border around an illustration.** "Where text borders white space is where you achieve emphasis." (Alley, p. 92) [firm]

## Captions and legends

**Introduce every illustration by its formal name.** Readers look at illustrations first and at captions before the text, so the caption has to work for someone who has not read the paragraph. (Alley, pp. 32-33) [firm]

**Start the caption with a title phrase that separates this illustration from every other one in the document.** Then answer the immediate questions the image raises. (Alley, pp. 32-33) [firm]

**Open a legend with a phrase naming the key point or comparison, then define symbols, panels, statistics, and just enough method.** Rarely more than two or three sentences. (Heard, pp. 116-117) [firm]

**Head tables above and set figure legends below.** Name and position are the only differences between the two. (Heard, p. 117; Alley, pp. 277, 283) [firm]

Heard and Alley pull slightly apart on length. Alley wants the caption to answer every immediate question the image raises; Heard caps a legend at two or three sentences. Apply Heard's cap and Alley's opening title phrase, and move anything that will not fit into the text.

A legend opening, weak then strong (example: ours):

- Weak: "Figure 3. Moran's I by census tract, 2010 to 2020."
- Strong: "Figure 3. Income clustering intensifies along the eastern corridor after 2015. Local Moran's I by census tract; filled symbols mark tracts significant at P < 0.05."

Heard asks a graphic to do two things at once: stand alone, and integrate with the text (p. 116). The caption is what makes the first half possible.

The author's float rules sit on top of these. Announce every float by name in the prose before it appears, claim its pattern by name where the reading lives, and drop generic warnings that panels differ in units when the axes already say so. See house-style.md.

## Connecting graphics to text

**Name the pattern first, then point to the graphic.** A pointer alone hands the reader your analysis job. Heard: "it's the writer's job, not the reader's, to extract pattern from data" (p. 112).

- Before: "See Table 1 for activation energies in the presence of different catalysts."
- After: "Activation energies were lowest on palladium catalysts (Table 1)." (Heard, p. 117) [firm]

**Never make one point by sending the reader to more than one graphic.** Heard's anti-model is a real sentence supporting a single claim about diet overlap with "(Fig. 2A-F, Figs. 3-6, Table 3)". His judgment: "This asks the reader to do some difficult data-analytic work . . . which the writer should have done instead." (p. 117) [firm]

**Match what the text says to what the illustration shows.** Alley's worked case is a test-system diagram: the text names five parts, the figure draws seven, and two of them are renamed between text and image. The fix redraws the figure to agree with the text, sets the tested object apart, and makes the flow visible. (Alley, pp. 89-90) [firm]

**Place each illustration after the paragraph that first refers to it, never before.** Scientific readers skim and read out of order, so a figure that appears before its introduction sends them backward for nothing. (Alley, p. 91; Appendix D, p. 277) [firm]

**Set a figure below the paragraph that introduces it, or at the top of the next page, and leave more space between the illustration and the text than between the illustration and its caption.** The spacing tells the reader which words belong to the image. (Alley, pp. 277, 283) [firm]

**Do not write "below" or "on the next page."** Revision invalidates the pointer, and Alley calls this "a rookie mistake" (p. 91). Point backward with the past tense instead: "as was shown in Figure 5-5." [firm]

## Equations inside sentences

**Make each equation a grammatical part of a sentence in a paragraph.** Alley's three conventions: define the dependent variable in the text before the equation; write the equation as a clause with a subject and a verb, where the equals sign is the verb; define the remaining independent variables after it. (Alley, p. 86) [firm]

**Treat an equation as part of the paragraph, not as a float.** An illustration stands outside the prose and carries its own caption; an equation stands inside a sentence and carries none. (Alley, pp. 86, 88) [firm]

**Give the reader a reason to work through it.** Every variable costs the reader a stop. So show why the equation matters, define every term, state its assumptions, give a numerical example, and convey the physical meaning. (Alley, pp. 86-88) [firm]

**Use Dirac's test for physical meaning.** "I understand what an equation means when I can predict the properties of its solution without actually solving it." (Dirac, quoted in Alley, p. 88) [firm]

**Do not assume a rigorous derivation communicates.** Alley states it plainly: "A rigorous mathematical derivation is not always the best way to communicate an equation" (p. 88). Derive when the derivation is the contribution; otherwise give the result, the assumptions, and the meaning. [firm]

**Center a displayed equation, set it apart with white space, and number in Arabic only the equations the text refers to.** A paragraph containing a displayed equation is the licensed exception to Alley's 15-line paragraph limit. (Alley, pp. 131, 276) [firm]

A worked form of the three conventions (example: ours):

> We estimate the local spatial association at site i as the product of its own standardized value and the weighted mean of its neighbors' standardized values:
>
>     I_i = z_i * sum_j ( w_ij * z_j )          (1)
>
> where z_i is the standardized attribute value at site i, w_ij is the row-standardized weight linking sites i and j, and the sum runs over the neighbors of site i.

The author's equation rules add three requirements on top of Alley's: one plain sentence naming the operation and its object before the equation, a stated provenance for every constant, and the formula paired with one concrete numerical interpretation. See house-style.md.

## Checklist

1. Does every set of numbers appear exactly once, in text, a table, or a figure, and never in two of them? (Heard, p. 105)
2. Do sentences carrying more than three numbers get converted to a graphic, and do graphics carrying two numbers get converted to a sentence? (Heard, p. 103)
3. In every table, do the familiar or independent variables sit left, and does the comparison run down a column? (Heard, pp. 108-109)
4. In every figure, do the vertical axes start at zero, do compared panels share a scale, and is any deviation stated? (Heard, p. 114)
5. Are codings, symbols, and panel conventions identical across all panels and all figures? (Heard, p. 112)
6. Is color doing work that nothing else could do? (Heard, p. 114)
7. Does every caption open with a title phrase that no other caption in the document could carry, and does it stand without the text? (Alley, pp. 32-33; Heard, pp. 116-117)
8. Does the text name the pattern before it cites the graphic, rather than saying "see Figure 2"? (Heard, p. 117)
9. Does any single claim send the reader to more than one graphic? (Heard, p. 117)
10. Does every part named in the text appear in the illustration under the same name? (Alley, pp. 89-90)
11. Does each illustration appear after the paragraph that introduces it, with no "below" or "next page" pointers? (Alley, pp. 91, 277)
12. Is every displayed equation a grammatical part of its sentence, with the dependent variable defined before it and the rest after, plus assumptions and one numerical example? (Alley, pp. 86-88)
